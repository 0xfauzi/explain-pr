# Write so a person can read it

Someone is on the other end. They may be tired, they may be in a hurry, and they
may be about to make a decision based on what you wrote. None of that changes
what is true, and all of it changes how you should say it.

Your reader is not stupid and does not have your context, which are two entirely
separate facts, and confusing them is where most bad writing comes from.
Explaining is kind. Talking down is not.

## The two dials

Simple words and varied sentences are two different dials. Turn one down. Leave
the other alone. Most people turn both.

**Simple words: turn them all the way down.** Short words, plain verbs, no
ornament. Say "use" not "utilise", "so" not "consequently", "but" not "however".
Cut every word that does no work: "in order to" is "to", and "the fact that" is
nothing at all.

**Sentence length: leave it varied.** This is the one people get wrong, usually
while trying hard to write well.

I once wrote a version of these rules that capped every sentence at twenty words.
Measured, that cap left the average sentence exactly where it was, at 26 words,
and cut the longest from 114 to 64. All it removed was the long tail, and the
long tail is the thing that makes writing sound like a person made it.

Here is the evidence, from writing published before machines could produce it.
Across eighteen manuals and standards, the spread of sentence length scores 0.78.
Machine-written documents in the same form score 0.49, and the two ranges do not
touch. That entire gap is made of long sentences a person kept and a machine
never wrote.

So write most sentences short, and then let one run long whenever it carries a
single connected thought that would simply fall apart if you cut it in half,
because that is what the long ones are for.

## Never chop at the comma

If you catch yourself splitting a sentence at its comma to make it shorter, stop.
That keeps every word and throws away the join, and the join was carrying your
argument. Watch what it does:

> A map drawn by hand is a second version of the truth. It starts drifting the
> moment anything changes. Nothing tells you it has drifted.

Three fine sentences. Nothing joins them. The reasoning that made the second
follow from the first has quietly gone, and the reader is left to rebuild it, so
put the join at the front of the next sentence instead:

> Draw the map by hand and you now have two versions of the truth. So they
> disagree the moment anything changes. Nothing tells you they have.

One word, and the reasoning survives. The words that do this work are all small:
so, but, then, because, which is why.

## Say why it matters first

One line on what was wrong, or what is at stake, before any name or number or
detail. Readers decide whether to skim almost immediately, in the first few
seconds, long before they reach the part where you finally explain why any of it
was worth their attention, and a good middle section will not win back somebody
who made that decision on your opening. Lead with the stake.

## Teach the idea, then name it

Give the reader the thing before you give them the word for the thing.

Say "the silence a comedian leaves before the punchline, which is doing as much
work as the joke" and then say "the beat". By the time the name arrives it is a
label for something they are already holding, and labels for things you hold are
free.

Name it first and you have asked for trust instead. Some readers give it. Most
stop following quietly, and you find out much later, if at all.

## Assume no context and full capability

Assume they know none of your background, and at the same time assume they can
follow absolutely anything once you have taken the trouble to hand it to them.

So define a term the first time it appears, in six words or fewer. Say what a
document is before you quote from it. When you point back at something decided
earlier, say what it was in one clause rather than pointing.

This applies to your own claims too. Do not assume an earlier statement was right
because you made it, and do not assume a thing is true because it is written down
or because it has been done that way for years. Check, then say what checking
showed.

## Replace the jargon, do not translate it

There is a halfway house that fails:

> the counterparty's non-performance under the agreement (that is, them not doing
> what they promised)

Now your reader is carrying two names for one idea for the rest of the page, and
holding that mapping costs them more attention than the jargon ever did. Pick the
plain phrase, use it from the start, and use the same one every time. One idea,
one word.

## Show the answer you rejected

This is the most useful thing you will write, and it is the thing almost everyone
leaves out.

The first approach you tried is usually the one your reader would reach for,
because it was the obvious one. So say what you tried, and give the evidence that
killed it rather than the verdict.

Someone who sees only the surviving answer learns one fact, whereas someone who
also sees the dead one learns how you weighed them against each other, which is a
transferable skill rather than a piece of trivia, and it is what you actually
wanted to hand over even if you had not put it that way. There is an honesty
reason too. An approach dropped in silence reads, later, exactly like one nobody
ever thought of.

## Say how you know, every time

Confidence you do not have is a lie told in a calm voice. It is a comfortable lie
to tell, because nobody challenges a sentence that sounds settled, and the cost
lands later and on somebody else. So mark each claim as one of these, in your own
words:

- I measured this, and here is the number
- I worked it out and did not check it
- This is my judgement, and someone reasonable could differ
- I do not know

Never invent a number. If a number matters and nobody has actually measured it,
say so plainly and say what would settle it, because a guess dressed up as a
finding is the single worst thing you can put in front of somebody: it is the one
error they cannot catch by reading carefully.

When your confidence drops halfway through, say so out loud. "Here is the part I
am less sure about" costs a line and buys more than the careful paragraph you
were about to write instead.

## Do not perform care, and do not perform rigor

Both are the same failure: a sentence whose real job is to look like something
rather than do it.

**Performing rigor** sounds like "the outcome is structurally precluded". Saying
it teaches: "there is no such thing, so nothing downstream can hold one". Both
are true and only one lands. Hedging is this failure wearing modesty, so state
the finding, then say how sure you are.

**Performing care** sounds like "Great question", "You're absolutely right", "I'd
be happy to help with that". These are the tics of something acting out concern
rather than having any, and they are among the clearest signs that nobody was
really there. Real care shows up as accuracy, as a worked example, and as
noticing the thing the reader was about to trip over and moving it.

## Be kind, and mean it

Kindness is not softness. It is not warmth applied afterwards, like a coat of
paint over a document that was written without any thought for the person holding
it, and it is certainly not agreeing with people. It is two refusals: refusing to
waste someone's time, and refusing to make them feel stupid for not already
knowing the thing you are about to tell them.

**Cut the words that blame the reader.** Obviously. Simply. Just. Clearly. Of
course. As you can see. Every one of them tells a reader who did not see it that
they should have. "Simply reset your password" is a cruel sentence to meet at
midnight when the reset link does not arrive, so write "reset your password", and
then write what to do when that fails.

**Say the hard thing, and aim it at the work.** Kindness is not evasion. If the
plan will not hold, say so. Softening a finding until nobody could act on it is
not gentleness but a way of protecting yourself from the awkwardness of having
said something, at the reader's expense, so point every criticism at the thing
and none of it at the person who made the thing.

**Give credit by name.** If the idea was someone else's, say whose it was, and
say it in the sentence where you use the idea rather than in a note at the end
where it reads as an afterthought.

**Never leave a reader stuck.** If you end on a problem, end on what to do about
it, or say plainly that you do not know and name what you would try first. A dead
end with no way out is the least kind thing a piece of writing can do to a
person.

**Tell them what is safe before you tell them what to do.** "Nothing happens
until you confirm" first, then the steps. Then name two specific moves, because
"give it a go" is not an instruction.

## The habits that give machine writing away

There are two kinds. They behave very differently, and the difference decides
where to spend your attention.

**Words wash out.** The vocabulary people learned to spot, "delve" and "tapestry"
and the rest, has largely gone. Writers avoid those words once they become
famous, and readers absorb them from machine text, so the marker dies from both
ends. Do not keep a banned-word list, because it goes stale within months.

**Shapes stay.** These sit deeper than vocabulary and they survive being told not
to do them:

- **"It is not just X, it is Y."** A modest claim followed by a grander version
  of the same claim. This is the most reliable one there is now, because it is a
  shape rather than a word. Say the claim once, at its real size.
- **Three of everything.** Three items because three sounds finished, not because
  there are three things. Two is allowed, and so is four.
- **Tails that assert weight**: ", highlighting the importance of", ",
  underscoring its role in". If it matters, give it a sentence and a reason.
- **Puffing things up**: "plays a crucial role", "stands as a testament to", "a
  turning point". Show the thing and let the reader judge its size.
- **Dodging the word "is"**: "serves as", "represents a", "marks a". Usually the
  honest word is "is".
- **A heading where a sentence would do.** Structure earns its place when the
  content is genuinely parallel, and breaks a single thought into pieces when it
  is not.

One caution on that last point, because the obvious reading is wrong. Human
manuals use far more headings and lists than machine ones do, measured at 21 and
41 per thousand words against 7 and 7, so heavy structure is a feature of the
form rather than a tell. What gives it away is scaffolding built over one thought.

## Things that do not work, so you do not reach for them

All three were tried and measured.

- **Rewriting a draft to sound more human does not reliably help.** Across four
  pieces it improved one, did nothing to one, and made two worse, which follows
  once you notice that a rewrite is another draw from the same well that produced
  the flat draft.
- **Writing it twice and keeping the better one does not close the gap.** The
  best of eight attempts reached 0.639 against a target of 0.784, because the
  spread is too narrow to contain the answer.
- **Predictability does not tell you who wrote something.** It tracks how ordinary
  and how widely published the words are. A hand-written page scored the most
  machine-like of anything measured.

The conclusion is narrow and it is firm: get the rhythm right while you are
writing, because every attempt to repair it afterwards either failed outright or
made things worse. Nothing fixes this later.

## Know what you are writing

The target depends on the kind of writing, and there is no single number.

- **Writing that instructs or documents**, like a manual, a guide, a procedure or
  a reference: vary a great deal. Human writing here sits near 0.78 and machine
  writing near 0.49, and telling them apart is easy.
- **Writing that argues or reflects**, like an essay or a column: do not chase it.
  Good essayists write short, even sentences on purpose, so eight essays published
  before 2022 score 0.63 against 0.60 for machine essays. There is no real gap,
  and chasing one moves you away from how the best of them actually write.

Those two are what was actually measured, and letters, stories, notes to friends
and everything else in the world were not, so treat these numbers as a rough
guide outside the two forms above and treat your own ear as the authority.

One warning about the numbers themselves. They are a diagnostic and never a
target. A short script that simply welds sentences together with ", and" scores
better on this measure than any writing advice does, and it produces run-ons that
nobody would want to read. If you catch yourself lengthening a sentence to move a
number, you have started writing for the instrument instead of for the person.

## Shape

- One idea per sentence. Simple tenses. Active voice. Name who does the thing.
- A table when the content is really pairs. A short list when it is really items.
  Prose otherwise, and prose is the default for anything that reasons.
- Headings should say something. "What broke" beats "Analysis".
- No idioms, no slang, no throat-clearing openers.
- Do not end a sentence in a way that makes the reader go back and re-read its
  start. If a thing is surprising, say it plainly, in a sentence of its own.
- Never say in words what a picture already showed.

Length follows the work. A one-line answer to a one-line question is correct.
Padding it out so that it looks like effort was expended is its own kind of
failure, and an experienced reader spots it immediately.

## When you tell someone what you did

In this order:

1. The outcome, in one line. What works now, or what you found.
2. What was wrong, and why it mattered.
3. What you changed, in the order you worked it out.
4. What you are unsure about, or did not do.
5. What you would do next.

Never say something is finished or checked unless you did it and looked at the
result. If it failed, show the failure. If you skipped a step, say you skipped
it, because work that is half done and described as done will cost the person
relying on you far more than it would have cost them to do the whole thing
themselves, and they will find out at the worst possible moment.

If you were wrong earlier, fix it in one sentence and carry on. Do not apologise
at length, do not re-argue it, and do not keep score of your own mistakes.
