## 1. Sources named in round 1 but not fetched: what they say when read

### Takeaway
Every named academic source was retrieved in full text, and they hold up, with three points the first pass could not see: the pre-agent productivity studies say almost nothing on quality or understanding; DORA 2025's own text gives 61% of respondents "never" using agent mode, so its findings are mostly about chat and autocomplete; and Li, Ko and Begel 2020 rank "pays attention to coding details" first of 54 attributes among 1,926 expert engineers. Vendor reports (Faros, CodeRabbit, Uplevel, JetBrains) were reachable only as landing pages, with methods gated.

### Cited Findings

**Over-trust and critical thinking**
- Perry, Srivastava, Kumar, Boneh (Stanford, CCS 2023). 47 participants analysed (33 with an AI assistant, 14 control) on five security tasks; 66% undergraduates, 19% graduate students, 15% professionals; 87% aged 18 to 24. Assistant was a Codex-based (codex-davinci-002) query tool. On the encryption task 67% of the AI group gave a correct solution against 79% of controls, and the AI group was significantly more likely to give an insecure solution (p = 0.017). On another task 3% of the AI group wrote a secure solution against 21% of controls (p = 0.039). "For every question, participants in the experiment on average believed their answers were more secure than those in the control group despite often providing more insecure answers." Participants with secure solutions trusted the AI less than those with insecure ones (Q3: 1.5 vs 4.0; Q2: 1.0 vs 3.53). [Type: controlled study; Tool: chat/completion, not agent; Pop.: mostly students; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate (small control group, students, 2022 model); Read: full text] — [arXiv 2211.03622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622)
- Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch, Drosos, Rintel, Banks, Wilson (CMU and Microsoft Research, CHI 2025). 319 knowledge workers recruited on Prolific who use generative AI at work at least weekly, sharing 936 task examples. Participants reported enacting critical thinking in about 60% of examples (555 of 936). Confidence in the AI doing the task was negatively associated with reported critical thinking (β = -0.69, p < 0.001); confidence in doing the task oneself (β = 0.26, p = 0.026) and in evaluating AI responses (β = 0.31, p = 0.046) were positively associated. Stated barriers to inspecting output included lack of time (44 of 319) and not having the knowledge to inspect (58 of 319). All measures are self-report; developers are one occupation group among many and are not analysed separately. [Type: survey; Tool: chat; Pop.: knowledge workers, mixed; Sells AI: yes (Microsoft); Strength: moderate for the association, weak for developers specifically; Read: full text] — [Lee et al. PDF](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)

**Pre-agent productivity experiments**
- Cui, Demirer, Jaffe, Musolff, Peng, Salz (Feb 2025 draft). Three company-run randomised field experiments (Microsoft, Accenture, an anonymous Fortune 100 firm), 4,867 developers, GitHub Copilot code completion. Instrumental-variable estimate: 26.08% more completed tasks per week (SE 10.3%), 13.55% more commits (SE 10.0%), 38.38% more builds (SE 12.55%). Gains were significant for recent hires and junior staff "but not for developers with longer tenure and in more senior positions". Quality was measured only by build success rate, with no negative effect found. Nothing on comprehension or review. [Type: controlled study (field); Tool: autocomplete; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: yes (two authors at Microsoft); Strength: moderate (large, randomised, but noisy, output proxies); Read: full text] — [MIT draft PDF](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/draft_copilot_experiments.pdf)
- Paradis et al. (Google, 2024). Randomised trial, 96 full-time Google engineers, one enterprise-grade C++ task, three AI features in the internal IDE (completion, smart paste, natural language to code). AI group averaged 96 minutes against 114 (t-test on log time p = .038); modelled effect "about 21%, although our confidence interval is large". The authors say the result may not carry over to other tools or times. Time only; no quality or understanding outcome. [Type: controlled study; Tool: autocomplete/chat; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: yes; Strength: moderate; Read: full text] — [arXiv 2410.12944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944)
- Uplevel. Blog page states: nearly 800 developers, Copilot access versus none, "no significant change in efficiency metrics", and the Copilot group "introduced 41% more bugs". Period, design and statistics are not on the page; the full study sits behind a separate resource link I did not obtain. [Type: observational data; Tool: autocomplete; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: sells engineering analytics; Strength: weak (method not visible); Read: page] — [Uplevel](https://uplevelteam.com/blog/ai-for-developer-productivity)

### Inferences
- The older experiments (Cui, Google, Perry) cannot bear weight for claims about agents. Two measure speed or output with completion tools; one measures security with a 2022 model and mostly students. Their use is as a baseline: output gains were already concentrated in juniors, and over-confidence was already measurable.
- DORA's small-batch finding is the best organisation-level support for "decompose and sequence" as a skill, and its own reading (AI inflates felt effectiveness through volume) fits the METR perception gap.
- Tang et al. give the first distribution of what goes wrong: breaking stated constraints and misreading intent together dominate, and the agent misreporting its own work is the third most common. Because the method only sees what the developer objected to, it is a floor on misalignment and says nothing about what was not noticed.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: Uplevel's full study; Faros's gated reports; JetBrains percentages; Stack Overflow 2026 field dates and total n; GitClear's full method.
- DORA 2026: I found no primary 2026 DORA State report. Secondary pieces mention a "DORA 2026 ROI report"; I could not verify it or any figure from it.
- None of the named sources measures understanding of agent-made changes.

## 2. Missing evidence, searched harder (2024 to November 2026)

### Takeaway
The harder search found partial evidence for five of the six gaps, and none that closes them. There is now telemetry on approval behaviour (auto-approve rises from about 20% to over 40% of Claude Code sessions with experience), repository data on review depth (90.6% of agent PRs in one dataset have no recorded review comments; review became faster and less detailed everywhere, not only where agents are used), and one small lab study where two of ten experienced developers' test results and understanding diverged. Still absent: any study of experienced engineers' understanding of agent changes on their own codebases, any measured skill change in working engineers, and any study tying low understanding to later cost.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Experienced engineers' understanding of agent-made changes**
- Qiao, Haque, Hundhausen (Oregon State, Sep 2026), "Say, Do, Understand". Ten developers with at least three years' professional experience (mean 6.5), GitHub Copilot in agent use, adding features to an unfamiliar frontend application for up to 2 hours 15 minutes, then graded on explaining the process and the solution. Performance and understanding agreed for eight and split for two: "one passed most tests but could not explain the solution, and another passed few tests but explained it well". Those who most often asked the agent to check its work spent the least time testing themselves. Authors: descriptive, does not generalise. [Type: observational lab study; Tool: agent; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: no; Strength: weak (n = 10, unfamiliar code, no control); Read: full text (abstract, method, participants)] — [arXiv 2609.33712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33712)
- Sahni et al. (Columbia, 12 Nov 2026), "Rasoi". A design probe that draws how the architecture changed after each agent edit, deployed for seven days with 12 developers on their own codebases. Motivation from formative interviews: participants "described difficulty noticing unexpected changes". Reported benefit: seeing whether changes stayed in scope and noticing unexpected restructuring. No comprehension measure and no comparison condition. [Type: interview/deployment study; Tool: agent; Pop.: developers on own code (experience mix not checked); Sells AI: no; Strength: weak for the problem's size, useful as direct testimony; Read: full text (abstract, method)] — [arXiv 2611.16293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16293)
- Chen, Talwalkar, Brennan, Neubig (CMU and All Hands AI, 2025), "Code with Me or for Me?". Within-participant comparison of GitHub Copilot and the OpenHands agent; participants mostly had 3 to 5 years' professional experience (60%) and no prior agent experience. Agents gave about 35% higher task correctness with about half the user effort; participants named "understanding of agent outputs" as a weakness. Understanding was not measured. [Type: controlled study; Tool: agent vs autocomplete/chat; Pop.: working engineers, early career; Sells AI: yes in part (All Hands AI builds OpenHands); Strength: weak for comprehension; Read: full text (abstract, participants)] — [arXiv 2507.08149](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08149)
- Kaufman, Brun, Murali, Endres (Jul 2026). 86 participants (66.3% professional developers) judged LLM-generated assertions about code: 74% accuracy on correct assertions, 49% on incorrect ones (p < 0.001), "despite reporting similar levels of confidence in both judgments". This tests a proposed shortcut (review the assertions, not the code) and finds people cannot reliably spot wrong ones. [Type: controlled study; Tool: LLM-generated artefacts, not an agent session; Pop.: mixed, majority professional; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate; Read: full text (abstract, results, participants)] — [arXiv 2607.08885](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08885)

**(b) Telemetry on acceptance without reading, review depth, time on agent diffs**
- Anthropic, "Measuring AI agent autonomy in practice" (18 Feb 2026). Claude Code sessions, late 2025 to early 2026. Full auto-approve is used in about 20% of sessions by users with fewer than 50 sessions, rising to over 40% by 750 sessions. Interrupts rise too, from about 5% of turns for new users to about 9% for experienced ones. On the public API (998,481 sampled tool calls), 73% of tool calls had a human in the loop in some way and 0.8% of actions appeared irreversible; these are model-made classifications that the authors treat as upper bounds. Stated limit: "limited visibility into downstream human review". Auto-approve is permission to act, so it measures approval before the fact, not whether the result was read. [Type: observational data; Tool: agent; Pop.: mixed; Sells AI: yes; Strength: moderate for approval behaviour, none for reading; Read: page] — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy)
- Baumann et al., SWE-chat (arXiv 2604.20779; Entire.io opt-in logs). In the version I downloaded: agents author more than 99% of committed code in 41.1% of sessions ("vibe coding"); humans write all code in 25.0%; 59.4% of agent-produced code survives into user commits (65.0% in vibe-coding sessions); users interrupt in 4.5% of turns and push back in 45.7%. The share of vibe-coding sessions "more than doubled, from under 20% of sessions in February to over 50%" recently. Earlier versions and press give different figures (44% survival, 23% human-only); the dataset is growing. About 69% of data is from Claude Code. [Type: observational data; Tool: agent; Pop.: mixed, early adopters who opt in; Sells AI: not checked; Strength: moderate; Read: full text (abstract, results passages)] — [arXiv 2604.20779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20779)

Round 1 had this figure as secondary; it is now verified. Caveat: many of these PRs are opened by the repository owner's own agent session, so the "reviewer" may be the person who prompted it. [Type: observational data; Tool: agent; Pop.: open source; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate; Read: full text] — [arXiv 2602.02345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02345)

[Type: observational data (quasi-experimental); Tool: agent; Pop.: open source; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate; Read: full text (abstract, results)] — [LIPIcs ESEM 2026.67](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.67)

**(c) Longitudinal skill change in working engineers**
- Vella and Blincoe (Auckland; TOSEM, arXiv v2 Nov 2026). Two questionnaires six months apart (October 2024 and April 2025), 158 and 101 eligible professional engineers, 95 matched. 82% reported less time writing code. Balance of work shifted significantly toward verification (adjusted p = 0.006). Flow problems rose from 54% to 76% of those reporting an experience problem. The authors propose "supervisory engineering work". Skill was not tested; everything is self-report; the period predates wide agent use. [Type: survey (longitudinal); Tool: mostly chat/autocomplete; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate for perceived work shift, none for skill; Read: full text (abstract, method, results passages)] — [arXiv 2605.23135](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23135)
- Sergeyuk et al. (JetBrains Research and UC Irvine, ICSE 2026). Two years of IDE telemetry from 800 developers plus a survey of 62. AI users' typed characters rose by 587 per month against 75 for non-users, and deletions by 102 per month against 7.6. Survey respondents mostly perceived no change in their editing. Behaviour, not skill; users self-select. [Type: observational data; Tool: in-IDE assistant, pre-agent; Pop.: working developers; Sells AI: yes (JetBrains); Strength: moderate for behaviour, none for skill; Read: full text (abstract, results passages)] — [arXiv 2601.10258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10258)
- Sankaranarayanan (Feb 2026), "epistemic debt". 78 novices (Prolific and UserInterviews), Cursor plugin with Claude 3.5 Sonnet, three arms. Both AI arms beat manual on function; in a later 30-minute maintenance task without AI, 77% of unrestricted-AI users failed against 39% of those who had to explain generated code before it could be integrated. Minutes, not months; novices. It is the only experiment found where a comprehension intervention changed a later outcome. [Type: controlled study; Tool: agent-capable IDE; Pop.: novices/students; Sells AI: no (author at a biotech firm); Strength: moderate for novices, weak for engineers; Read: full text (abstract, participants)] — [arXiv 2602.20206](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20206)

**(d) Low understanding linked to later cost**
- Borg et al. (CodeScene, Equal Experts, Lund; "Echoes of AI", v3 Feb 2026). Preregistered two-phase experiment, 151 participants, 95% professional. Phase 1: add a feature to a Java web app with or without AI assistants. Phase 2 (randomised): different developers evolve those solutions without AI. "No significant differences in subsequent evolution with respect to completion time or code quality." This tests whether AI-assisted code is harder for others to maintain, and finds no sign of it at code level; it does not test what the original author understood, and it used assistants, not autonomous agents. [Type: controlled study; Tool: assistant (Copilot, Cursor); Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: CodeScene sells code-quality tools; Strength: moderate to strong for its narrow question; Read: full text (abstract)] — [arXiv 2507.00788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00788)
- He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner, Vasilescu (CMU, MSR 2026), now read in full text. 806 repositories adopting Cursor, matched controls. Lines added +28.6% on average, concentrated in the first two months (+281.3% then +48.4%); commits +55.4% then +14.5%; no lasting velocity effect. Static analysis warnings +30.3% and code complexity +41.6%, both persistent. Panel model: a 100% rise in complexity causes a 64.5% fall in later velocity, and in warnings a 50.3% fall. This links accumulated code-level cost to later slowdown; human understanding is not measured. [Type: observational data (quasi-experimental); Tool: agent-capable IDE; Pop.: open source; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate to strong; Read: full text] — [arXiv 2511.04427](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04427)
- Sawada et al. (May 2026): in repositories with agent-generated files, 83.21% of later commits to those files were made by humans and 16.79% by agents. Humans still do most maintenance of agent-written code. [Type: observational data; Tool: agent; Pop.: open source; Sells AI: no; Strength: weak to moderate; Read: full text (results passage)] — [arXiv 2605.06464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06464)

**(e) Pull requests written by agents**
- Ehsani et al. (MSR 2026), 33,596 agent-authored PRs in repositories with more than 100 stars. Overall merge rate 71.48%; Codex 82.59%, Cursor 65.22%, Claude Code 59.04%, Devin 53.76%, Copilot 43.04%. By task: documentation 84%, CI 79%, build 74%; performance 55%, fix 64%. Each failed CI check lowers merge odds by about 15%. In a hand-coded sample of 600 unmerged PRs the largest category was reviewer abandonment (228, 38%: no meaningful human engagement), then duplicates (23%), CI or test failure (17%); only 13 (2%) were agent misalignment. [Type: observational data; Tool: agent; Pop.: open source; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate; Read: full text] — [arXiv 2601.15195](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15195)
- Pinna, Gong, Williams, Sarro (MSR 2026), 7,156 PRs: documentation 82.1% accepted against 66.1% for new features. **Correction**: round 1 recorded a "29 point gap"; the abstract I read gives a 16 percentage point gap. [Read: full text (abstract)] — [arXiv 2602.08915](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08915)
- Cynthia et al. (Jul 2026): 54,791 agent-generated review comments in 342 Python repositories; among unresolved discussions the most common reasons were incorrect suggestions and intentional design decisions the agent did not know about. [Type: observational data; Read: full text (abstract)] — [arXiv 2607.21997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21997)
- Reverts of agent PRs: I found no study measuring revert rates for agent-authored PRs. The Cursor-sponsored study below reports that PR revert rate "did not significantly change" at organisation level.

**(f) Usage research from other vendors**
- OpenAI with Columbia, Wharton and Duke authors, "The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex" (25 Jun 2026). Active users grew more than fivefold in the first half of 2026. "More than 10% of users manage three or more concurrent Codex agents at some point each week"; 26.6% use skills. The share of individual users submitting at least one request estimated at more than eight hours of expert human work rose nearly tenfold since January. The paper reports usage and volume; in the passages I read it has no measure of review, acceptance or understanding. [Type: observational data; Tool: agent; Pop.: mixed, including non-developers; Sells AI: yes; Strength: moderate for usage; Read: full text (abstract only in effect)] — [arXiv 2606.26959](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26959)
- Cursor blog on Suproteem Sarkar's study (University of Chicago; SSRN 5713646). "Tens of thousands" of users; organisations merged 39% more PRs after the agent became default, relative to a baseline group; revert rate unchanged; bugfix rate "slightly decreased". Each standard deviation of experience goes with about 6% higher acceptance of agent output; more experienced developers are more likely to plan before generating. About 61% of conversation-starting requests in a 1,000-user sample were for implementation. Period and organisation count are not on the page; I did not obtain the paper. [Type: observational data; Tool: agent; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: yes (Cursor publishes; author is academic); Strength: weak to moderate until the paper is read; Read: page] — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/productivity)
- Microsoft: the CLI rollout study in section 1. GitHub and Google: I found no first-party usage study of Copilot coding agent or Gemini agents comparable to Anthropic's. The figure that 75% of new code at Google is AI-generated appears as a citation to Business Insider inside an academic paper; I did not verify it. — [arXiv 2607.13196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13196)

### Inferences
- Experienced users grant more blanket permission and interrupt more often. That is consistent with supervising by exception: watch the run, stop it when it goes wrong, do not approve each step. It moves the point of human judgment from "before each action" to "during and after", which is where a comprehension aid would have to work.
- Acceptance and experience now point in the opposite direction from the autocomplete era: seniors accept more agent output (Cursor), adopt agents more (Microsoft), and plan more first. Nobody has tested whether they accept more because they evaluate better or because they scope tasks better.
- Review got thinner across open source whether or not a repository used agents (Hamza). So thin review of agent PRs may be part of a general change, and "agents caused shallow review" is not supported by the one study with a control group.
- The Borg result is a real counterweight: at code level, AI-assisted code was not harder for strangers to change. If there is a cost of not understanding, the evidence places it in system-level complexity over months (He et al.) and in operations (section 5), not in the readability of individual changes.
- The Sankaranarayanan and Balepur results agree on one design fact: making people engage with generated code (explain it, or accept file by file) goes with better later performance. Both are small and on novices or students.

### Gaps
- (a) Still no study measuring experienced engineers' understanding of agent-made changes in codebases they own. The nearest are ten people on unfamiliar code and a 12-person tool probe.
- (b) Still no telemetry on whether diffs from agents are opened or read, time spent per diff, or defects missed. Approval settings and comment counts are proxies.
- (c) Still no measurement of skill (as opposed to reported time use or keystrokes) in working engineers over time.
- (d) Still no study that measures a person's or team's understanding and then a later cost. Storey lists candidate measures; none has been used.
- (e) No revert or post-merge defect study of agent PRs found.
- (f) The Harvard/Jellyfish paper and Sarkar's SSRN paper were not obtained; their figures are secondary or vendor-relayed. The Harvard figures differ between versions.
- Downloaded but not read: arXiv 2607.21832 (agentic PR contributions), 2603.15911 (human-AI review synergy), 2605.10702 (ChatGPT on unfamiliar code), 2604.00299, 2602.09185 (AIDev dataset paper), 2606.20882, 2609.24234 beyond its abstract and framework (19 experienced developers; seven supervision stages: plan, monitor, wait, review, teach, manual fix, update assets; no time measurements).

## 3. Software architects, architecture knowledge, seniority, review and incident response

### Takeaway
What I could read supports two points: practitioners name loss of architecture knowledge and changes whose side effects were not anticipated among the main causes of erosion, with "hard to understand and maintain" as the first consequence; and review usefulness falls as changes get larger and rises steeply with a reviewer's first year of context. Measured outcome differences between senior and junior engineers, and measured predictors of good incident response, remain thin in what I could retrieve.

### Cited Findings
- Li, Liang, Soliman, Avgeriou (ICPC 2021), architecture erosion from practitioners' view. Online community posts plus a survey of 10 and interviews with 4 (13 of 38 contacted practitioners responded, 34.2%). Twelve cause types; the most frequent is "inappropriate architecture changes", where "developers cannot fully understand which part of the functionality will be implicated". Others: design defects, lack of management skills, knowledge vaporization ("mostly due to developer" turnover, leading to "the lack of knowledge about the current architecture"), lack of communication, lack of maintenance. Most-mentioned consequence: "hard to understand and maintain"; then run-time quality degradation. No costs are quantified. [Type: interview study and survey; Tool: none; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: no; Strength: weak to moderate (small, perceptions); Read: full text (causes and consequences section)] — [arXiv 2103.11392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.11392)
- Li, Soliman, Liang, Avgeriou (2022), erosion symptoms in code review. 21,274 review comments from OpenStack Nova and Neutron checked by hand; 502 discuss erosion. Most frequent symptoms: architectural violation, duplicate functionality, cyclic dependency. Most reviews that raise a symptom lead to its removal. So reviewers do catch system-level drift, rarely, and when they do it usually gets fixed. [Type: observational data; Tool: none; Pop.: open source; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate; Read: full text (abstract)] — [arXiv 2201.01184](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01184)
- Rehman, Mirakhorli, Nagappan, Uulu, Thornton (ICSE 2018), hands-on architects in five industrial systems. Implementing architectural tactics is more complex and more error-prone than ordinary functionality, and "architects tend to introduce fewer bugs into the implementation of architectural tactics compared to the developers". The authors present this as support for architects writing code. I read only the lab's publication page; bug counts are not on it. [Type: observational data plus interviews; Tool: none; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate, pending the full paper; Read: page] — [Waterloo SWAG](https://swag.uwaterloo.ca/publications/roles-and-impacts-of-hands-on-software-architects-in-five-industrial-case-studies.html)
- Bosu, Greiler, Bird (Microsoft, MSR 2015). About 1.5 million review comments from five projects. The share of a reviewer's comments that are useful "increases dramatically in the first year" at Microsoft and then plateaus; "the more files that are in a change, the lower the proportion of comments" that are useful. [Type: observational data; Tool: none; Pop.: working engineers; Sells AI: pre-AI Microsoft research; Strength: moderate to strong; Read: page] — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/characteristics-of-useful-code-reviews-an-empirical-study-at-microsoft/)
- Baltes and Diehl (FSE 2018). Survey of 335 developers and a literature synthesis: "experience is not necessarily related to expertise"; they cite prior work that length of experience is "only a weak correlate of job performance after the first two years". [Type: survey; Tool: none; Pop.: working developers; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate; Read: full text (abstract and one passage)] — [arXiv 1807.06087](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06087)
- Microsoft 2026 rollout study, survey quote on seniority and agents: the tools are "an especially good fit for experienced, senior developers, who can break work down into smaller chunks that the AI [...] can implement[...] and then review[...]", with doubt that juniors "who 'don't know what they don't know'" can use them as well. Opinion from one respondent, not a measured difference. [Read: full text] — [arXiv 2607.01418](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01418)

### Inferences
- Bosu's two results translate directly: a reviewer without context on the code gives less useful review, and bigger changes get worse review. Agent work produces both conditions at once (larger PRs per Faros and DORA's hypothesis, and code the reviewer did not write).
- The erosion studies describe, before AI, the same failure the comprehension-debt writers describe now: changes made without knowing what they implicate, and knowledge that leaves with people. What is new is the rate, which is asserted (Storey) and partly visible in complexity data (He et al.).
- "Seniority" is a poor stand-in for skill (Baltes and Diehl). A tool that targets skills should not assume years of experience supply them.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved (blocked or behind publisher walls): Bacchelli and Bird 2013 (403), Sadowski et al. 2018 on code review at Google (403), Ernst et al. 2015 on architectural technical debt (401), the ACM Queue article on debugging incidents at Google (403). Semantic Scholar's API timed out. Nothing is quoted from these.
- No empirical study of what architects do day to day was read. The architecture-erosion mapping study (arXiv 2112.10934) was downloaded but not read.
- Architectural technical debt costs (for example the often-quoted share of developer time lost to technical debt) were not verified this session; no figures given.
- I found no study that measures what predicts good incident response. Unknown.
- I found no study with measured outcome differences between senior and junior engineers working with agents. The Cursor and Microsoft findings are about acceptance and adoption, not outcomes.

## 4. The dispute about code fluency

### Takeaway
The evidence leans against the claim that system-level skill can be held without code-level fluency, but it is indirect. Expert engineers rank code-level competence first; architects who code make fewer errors in architectural code; non-developers building with agents are the only group that reports giving up because they cannot understand the output; and the capacity to check agent work tracks how much a person codes without AI. The strongest evidence on the other side is that great engineering managers are not expected to write code, yet even there they are expected to comprehend it.

### Cited Findings

**For the claim that fluency is needed**
- 1,926 expert engineers rank "pays attention to coding details" first of 54 attributes (63.1% essential). [Read: full text] — [Li, Ko, Begel 2020](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Li2019WhatDistinguishesEngineers.pdf)
- Architects who write code introduce fewer bugs in architectural tactics than developers do (five industrial systems). [Read: page] — [Rehman et al. 2018](https://swag.uwaterloo.ca/publications/roles-and-impacts-of-hands-on-software-architects-in-five-industrial-case-studies.html)
- Fawzy, Tahir, Blincoe (May 2026). Survey of 162 vibe coders in three groups (non-coders, novices, professionals). Perceptions of code quality were similar across groups, but non-developers reported more reprompting in place of debugging than professionals (adjusted p = .008) and more "QA breakdown or confusion", defined as giving up on debugging "because they cannot understand the AI-generated code" (versus novices p = .027, versus professionals p = .002). Non-developers "were the only group to report never checking AI-generated code prior to use"; 46.3% of them never code without vibe coding. In regressions, group membership stopped predicting once usage was included: "greater independent coding activity (coding without vibe coding)" predicted less reprompting and more checking. The authors call this a "perception-action gap": awareness of risk is evenly spread, the capacity "to evaluate, debug, and verify remains experience-dependent". [Type: survey; Tool: agent/vibe coding; Pop.: mixed including non-developers; Sells AI: no; Strength: moderate (self-report, n = 162); Read: full text] — [arXiv 2605.24521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24521)
- Chou et al. (UC Irvine and others, FSE 2026). Grounded theory from 20 vibe-coding videos (7 live sessions of about 16 hours and 254 prompts; 13 opinion videos). "Vibe coders with limited technical backgrounds, or who never examined the code, tended to provide instructions and sometimes introduced errors." Examples: one could not prompt well because "I don't know enough about 3D game engines to tell it how to"; one thought they were building a mobile app when it was a website; one proposed adding delays to database writes to fix a race condition instead of locks. Those who had read the code could write precise prompts naming components. [Type: observational/qualitative; Tool: agent; Pop.: mixed; Sells AI: no; Strength: weak to moderate (20 public videos); Read: full text (relevant section)] — [arXiv 2512.22418](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22418)
- Sankaranarayanan: novices who shipped working code with unrestricted AI failed a later maintenance task 77% of the time. [Read: full text (abstract)] — [arXiv 2602.20206](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20206)
- AIRELI: the eight "AI-steered" students finished with AI and none finished without it. [Read: full text] — [AIRELI preprint](https://aireli.hasel.dev/download/aireli-preprint.pdf)

**For the claim that system-level judgment can sit above code**
- Kalliamvakou, Bird, Zimmermann, Begel, DeLine, German (TSE 2019), great engineering managers at Microsoft. 37 interviews and 563 survey responses. "Being technical" ranked 14th of 15 attributes. By respondents' account the manager "as a rule, does not produce technical output, i.e. does not write code" and does not make the technical decisions. But "being technical" was still expected, defined as comprehension: enough knowledge "to understand the engineers' work, the tools and technologies they are working with, and the system they are building", to "ask the right questions about why one [alternative] is preferable to another", arbitrate design dilemmas and set quality standards "through code reviews and feedback". [Type: interview study and survey; Tool: none; Pop.: working engineers and managers; Sells AI: pre-AI Microsoft research; Strength: moderate; Read: full text] — [Kalliamvakou et al.](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/kalliamvakou-tse-2018.pdf)
- Storey records the position without endorsing it: understanding "may not need to be about details of the implementation layer", and some argue it "is often not needed at all, similarly to how managers have always had to trust team members". [Type: opinion; Read: full text] — [arXiv 2603.22106](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22106)
- More experienced developers accept more agent output and plan more before generating (Cursor data). This shows that experience changes how people work above the code, but the experienced group can all read code, so it does not separate the two skills. [Read: page] — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/productivity)

### Inferences
- The manager study is the closest thing to a natural experiment on "review-only" roles, and its answer is specific: managers stop producing code but are still expected to read and judge it. That supports a distinction between writing fluency (which may matter less) and reading or evaluating fluency (which every source treats as required).
- The vibe-coding surveys suggest that what protects is ongoing practice of coding without the agent, not a job title. If that holds, system-level skill built on top of no code practice would be expected to show the non-developer pattern: aware of risk, unable to act on it.
- None of these studies tests the direct question: can someone with strong system and design skill and no code fluency supervise agent work to a good outcome? The claim is neither confirmed nor refuted by experiment.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study of "architecture astronauts" or of architects who stopped coding with measured outcomes, other than Rehman et al., which I read only as an abstract page.
- No study of review-only roles (people who review but never write) was found.
- No study follows non-programmers building with agents over months to see where projects fail; the evidence is surveys and videos.
- Vibe-coding papers downloaded but not read: arXiv 2509.12491, 2506.23253.

## 6. The human share of work with agents

### Takeaway
No study measures a full breakdown of developer time across planning, specifying, reviewing, waiting and fixing. The pieces that exist agree on direction: less time writing, more supervising and verifying, a distinct "wait" state used to start other work, a growing minority running several agents at once, and about half of turns spent pushing back on output.

### Cited Findings
- Self-reported time shift over six months (95 matched engineers, October 2024 to April 2025): 82% report less time writing code; reviewing was the only task rated above neutral at both points; the balance moved significantly toward verification (adjusted p = 0.006). The authors expected more people to report more verification time and suggest developers "may not recognise the verification they perform" inside the interaction. [Type: survey; Tool: mostly chat/autocomplete; Pop.: working engineers; Strength: moderate; Read: full text] — [Vella and Blincoe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23135)
- Supervision stages from observing 19 developers with at least three years' experience: plan, monitor, wait, review, teach, manual fix, update assets. "Wait" is distinct from "monitor": during it developers supervise other agents or do unrelated work. Developers manage load by "concentrating effort in planning, delegating supervisory work to other agents, and turning recurring guidance into reusable assets". No durations measured. ### Inferences
- The human contribution is concentrating in three places: before the run (plan and specify), at interruption points during it, and at acceptance after it. Roughly every second turn involves the human correcting course, so "delegation" in practice is still a tight loop for most users.
- The fact that the most common request to an agent is to explain existing code suggests developers already use agents as a comprehension aid, unprompted.
- Concurrency breaks the measurement of time per task (METR said so in round 1) and also divides attention at the review point. More than one in ten Codex users are in that position weekly.

### Gaps
- No measured split of developer time across planning, specifying, reviewing, waiting and fixing exists in anything I read. Waiting time is measured nowhere.
- No data on how the split differs by seniority beyond the Cursor planning and acceptance findings.
- A Linear data write-up claiming planning time did not move while output tripled surfaced in search; not read, unverified.

## What is still unknown after this pass

- Whether experienced engineers understand agent-made changes to systems they own, and how often they do not. Unmeasured.
- How much agent output is merged unread. Only proxies exist (auto-approve settings, zero-comment PRs, self-report near 45% to 48% "always check").
- Whether working engineers lose skill with agent use. No measurement of skill over time exists.
- Whether lower understanding causes later cost. The one maintainability experiment found no code-level penalty; the one repository study found a complexity penalty; neither measured understanding.
- Whether system-level skill can be exercised without code-reading fluency. Indirect evidence says no; no direct test exists.
- What predicts good incident response or good supervision of agents at the level of the individual. Not found.
