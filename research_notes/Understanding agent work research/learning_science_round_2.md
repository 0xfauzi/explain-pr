# Learning science, round 2: gaps from the first pass, direct evidence, transfer, long-run use, diagrams

Notes dated 16 November 2026. These notes fill the gaps listed in `learning_science.md` (round 1). They do not repeat round 1 findings unless a number there is now verified or corrected.

**How to read these notes**

- Every finding carries: source type, population, strength as applied to this tool (experienced engineers, system-level judgment, months), and a reading tag.
- Reading tags (how I read the source in this session):
  - `[FULL]` = I downloaded the full text (PDF or PubMed Central XML), extracted the text, and read the abstract plus the results and discussion passages I quote. I did not read every page of every paper.
  - `[ABSTRACT]` = I read only the abstract in the primary source or its repository record.
  - `[SECONDARY]` = I saw the claim only in a search-engine summary or in a paper that cites the source. Treat numbers as unverified.
- Strength scale is the same as round 1: Strong, Moderate, Weak, Very weak, judged for *this tool*, not for the finding in its home setting.
- "STUDENT-RECALL" marks findings that hold for students on recall or comprehension tests and have not been shown for professional judgment.
- Effect sizes are quoted only where I read them in the source this session, unless tagged `[SECONDARY]`.
- Two sources carry 2026 arXiv dates that fall after my training data; I read their text this session and report only what the text says.

## 1. Sources the first pass could not retrieve, now read

### Takeaway
Eleven of the fourteen named sources were read in full text and the numbers quoted from memory in round 1 are now verified or corrected: the generation effect is d = 0.40 but near zero for unfamiliar material and smaller between subjects; the expertise reversal pooled effect is d = 0.505 for novices and −0.428 for the more knowledgeable; productive failure is g = 0.36 but zero for procedural knowledge and negative for domain-general skills; feedback is d = 0.48 with high-information feedback far ahead of bare right/wrong signals. Three sources stayed out of reach (Fiorella and Mayer 2016, Letovsky 1987, Schmeck et al. 2014).

### Cited Findings

**Dunlosky et al. 2013**
- Ten learning techniques rated for utility. High: practice testing, distributed practice. Moderate: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. Low: summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading (Table 4). The round 1 memory of these ratings was correct. Type: expert narrative review (monograph). Population: students. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength for this tool: Moderate as a ranking of techniques; Weak for professionals. `[FULL]` — [Dunlosky et al. 2013, WKU copy](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Self-explanation fell short of "high" because durability over educationally relevant delays and efficacy in representative settings were not established, and the authors flag that its time cost ("bang for the buck") needs study. Table 4 rates its implementation issues "Qualified" and educational-context evidence "Insufficient". `[FULL]` — [same source](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)

**Expertise reversal: Tetzlaff et al. 2025 and Kalyuga 2007**
- Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters and Brod (2025): 60 studies, 176 effect sizes (88 per prior-knowledge level), study sample sizes 17 to 357 (median 73), published 1992 to 2024. Low-prior-knowledge learners learn more with high-assistance instruction, d = 0.505 (95% CI 0.260 to 0.750). High-prior-knowledge learners learn *less* with high assistance than with low, d = −0.428 (95% CI −0.647 to −0.209). The within-study difference of the two is d = 0.971 (95% CI 0.631 to 1.312). Heterogeneity is very high (I² = 90.87% and 87.55%). Egger tests not significant (no evidence of publication bias). Type: meta-analysis. Population: school, vocational and university learners. Strength: Moderate for the phenomenon; Weak for ten-year engineers. `[FULL]` — [Tetzlaff et al. 2025, peDOCS open copy](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2026/34113/pdf/Learn_and_Instr_2025_Tetzlaff_u.a._A_cornerstone_of_adaptivity.pdf)
- The effect is not symmetric: the authors state that giving novices assistance helps more than withholding redundant assistance from experts, and that "the instructional implication is to rather provide assistance than to withhold it when in doubt". `[FULL]` — [same source](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2026/34113/pdf/Learn_and_Instr_2025_Tetzlaff_u.a._A_cornerstone_of_adaptivity.pdf)
- Moderator that matters for this tool: how "expert" was defined. For high-prior-knowledge learners the harm from assistance was strongest when groups were defined by grade level (reference estimate −0.95) and "weakest when defined by measurement or intervention"; when defined by profession or field of study the estimate did not differ significantly from the grade-level reference (difference −0.08, p = 0.77; 8 studies, 18 effect sizes). Vocational training had only 3 studies. Dichotomising a continuous prior-knowledge measure is named as a limitation. `[FULL]` — [same source](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2026/34113/pdf/Learn_and_Instr_2025_Tetzlaff_u.a._A_cornerstone_of_adaptivity.pdf)
- Kalyuga (2007) is a narrative review inside cognitive load theory with no pooled estimate and no moderator analysis; Tetzlaff et al. report that the simple average difference across Kalyuga's 26 studies was 1.72 and explain their lower value by proper weighting. Kalyuga's stated mechanism: guidance that is essential for novices becomes redundant for the more knowledgeable and costs them working-memory effort; he notes prior research used "mostly well-defined technical domains". Type: narrative review. Population: school pupils, university students, trade trainees. Strength: Weak for this tool. `[FULL]` (abstract, tables and conclusion read) — [Kalyuga 2007, archived course copy](https://web.archive.org/web/2id_/https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)

**Generation effect: Bertsch et al. 2007**
- 445 effect sizes, 86 studies, 17,711 participants: overall d = 0.40 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.42). The round 1 figure is now verified. Moderators read in the paper: within-subjects designs 0.50 versus between-subjects 0.28; recognition 0.46, cued recall 0.55, free recall 0.32; incidental learning 0.65 versus intentional 0.32; by retention interval, immediate 0.41, under 1 minute 0.32, 1 minute to 1 day 0.41, more than 1 day 0.64 (30 effect sizes); by stimulus, numbers 0.87, words 0.41, **nonwords 0.05**. Type: meta-analysis. Population: 427 of 445 effects from younger adults, mostly college students; word-list materials. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Moderate for item memory; Weak for this tool. `[FULL]` — [Bertsch et al. 2007, author copy](https://mcdaniel97.github.io/Publications/Bertsch%20et%20al.%202007.pdf)

**Productive failure: Sinha and Kapur 2021**
- 53 studies, 166 comparisons. Problem solving before instruction beats instruction first for conceptual knowledge and transfer, g = 0.36. For **procedural knowledge the pooled effect is g = −0.03** (p = .74, 95% CI −0.20 to 0.15; 51 comparisons). By grade: second to fifth graders −0.09, sixth to tenth graders 0.50, undergraduates 0.28. **Domain-general skills −0.17** (favouring instruction first). The p-curve estimate "in absence of publication bias" is g = 0.87. Only 5 of 166 comparisons (3%) involved postgraduates or professionals, and the authors call for more studies with them. Type: meta-analysis. Population: school and university students. Strength: Moderate for conceptual learning in students; Weak for professionals. `[FULL]` — [Sinha and Kapur 2021, archived publisher PDF](https://web.archive.org/web/2id_/https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- The authors' explanation of the domain-general reversal is directly relevant: when the problem-solving phase gives no implicit feedback about which actions were failures, learners cannot use awareness of their gaps in the later instruction, so attempt-first "can therefore be expected to perform worse". `[FULL]` — [same source](https://web.archive.org/web/2id_/https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/00346543211019105)

**Feedback: Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie 2020**
- 435 studies, 994 effect sizes, more than 61,000 participants. Overall d = 0.55, falling to d = 0.48 after removing extreme values; 17% of effects were negative; I² = 86%. Controlled studies d = 0.42 versus pre-post designs 0.63. By outcome: cognitive 0.51, motivational 0.33, physical 0.63, behavioural 0.48 (CI includes zero). By type: reinforcement or punishment 0.24, corrective feedback 0.46, **high-information feedback 0.99** (42 effects). High-information feedback tells the learner not only what was wrong but why and what to do next. Of the negative motivational effects, 86% came from uninformative feedback (rewards or punishments). Type: meta-analysis. Population: students in educational settings (non-educational settings were excluded). STUDENT-RECALL for the most part. Strength: Moderate. `[FULL]` — [Wisniewski et al. 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/pdf)

**Forecasting: Mellers et al. 2014**
- Two-year tournament, 199 geopolitical questions, entry required a bachelor's degree; participants averaged age 36, 64% with postgraduate training; Year 1 began with 2,246 participants, random assignment to conditions (about 177 per survey condition). Training took about 45 minutes at the start of the year and could be re-read. Training improved Year 1 Brier scores, F(2, 1586) = 14.29, p < .001; probability training beat scenario training, which beat none. In Year 2, probability training still beat no training, F(1, 882) = 19.12, p < .001. Calibration improved with training in both years (F = 3.16, p = .04; F = 3.78, p < .05). All main effects held under Brier, logarithmic and spherical scoring. The paper gives no single percentage improvement in the passages I read; the "about 10%" figure remains from Tetlock et al. (round 1). Type: randomized experiments in a field tournament. Population: educated adult volunteers, paid $150 to $250 a year, with leaderboards. Strength: Moderate to Strong for discrete resolvable events. `[FULL]` — [Mellers et al. 2014, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4rg4n9vr/qt4rg4n9vr.pdf)

**Drawing: Van Meter and Garner 2005; Schmeck et al. 2014**
- Van Meter and Garner reviewed 15 empirical studies and drew three conclusions: (1) the accuracy of the learner's drawing predicts later performance (a significant positive correlation in every study that scored accuracy); (2) learners need support to draw effectively; (3) benefits show on higher-order assessments, not on lower-order recognition tests. They stress a gap between classroom advocacy of drawing and the evidence. Type: narrative review. Population: school pupils mainly. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Weak for this tool. `[FULL]` — [Van Meter and Garner 2005, open copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-promise-and-practice-of-learner-generated-drawing-ioq0lo8in0.pdf)
- Schmeck et al. (2014): not retrieved in full. A search summary of the abstract reports two experiments with eighth-graders reading a text on influenza; drawing versus reading only gave d = 0.85 on comprehension and d = 1.15 on a drawing test, and drawing accuracy correlated with comprehension (r = .623 and .470). A British Psychological Society digest reports the learner-drawing group also beat a group given author-made drawings (63% versus 53% correct), with testing immediately after study. Type: controlled experiments. Population: 13- to 14-year-olds. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Weak. `[SECONDARY]` — [Leuphana record](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/drawing-pictures-during-learning-from-scientific-text-testing-the/); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/want-learn-something-better-draw-it)

**ICAP: Chi and Wylie 2014**
- A framework paper, not a meta-analysis. It classifies activities by overt behaviour as passive, active, constructive or interactive and predicts learning rises in that order. It lists "generating predictions", self-explaining and drawing concept maps as constructive. The authors acknowledge that overt behaviour is "far from perfect" as an index of thinking, that they have not verified the assumed knowledge-change processes, and that cognitive load theory can make the opposite prediction. Evidence cited is from note taking, concept mapping and self-explaining in students. Type: theory with supporting review. Population: students. Strength: Weak as a design rule. `[FULL]` (framework, taxonomy and caveats sections read) — [Chi and Wylie 2014](https://chilab.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf)

**Program comprehension classics**
- Pennington (1987), read in its technical-report version: Study 1 tested 80 professional programmers (40 COBOL, 40 FORTRAN; minimum three years' experience; about 10 years as professionals on average) on 15-line program texts; Study 2 had 40 of them study and modify a 200-line program. Result: procedural (control-flow) relations, not functional ones, dominate the first mental representation; task goals shape later representations. In Study 2 the top comprehension quartile made about 40% errors and the bottom quartile about 60%. The "situation model" (a representation in terms of the real-world domain) is introduced in the discussion as an explanation the author calls speculative, needing further research. Type: controlled experiments. Population: professional programmers. Strength: Moderate as description of first-pass comprehension of short programs; Very weak for system-level understanding. `[FULL]` (report version; wording may differ from the journal article) — [Pennington, DTIC report ADA179392, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2id_/https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA179392.pdf)
- Letovsky (1987) itself was not retrieved. Two sources I did read describe the same protocol data. Letovsky and Soloway (1986): six professional programmers (four with 3 to 20 years' experience, two with under three) were videotaped thinking aloud while adding a feature to an existing program; three of six finished in the allotted 90 minutes; the paper shows comprehension going wrong when code implementing one plan is scattered ("delocalized") and readers rely on local clues. Von Mayrhauser and Vans (1995) summarise Letovsky's model: why, how and what conjectures held with varying certainty, and an assimilation process that is opportunistic between top-down and bottom-up. Type: think-aloud study, n = 6. Population: professionals. Strength: Weak. `[FULL]` for the 1986 paper; `[SECONDARY]` for the 1987 paper — [Letovsky and Soloway 1986](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs69995-PC/papers/letovsky-1986-software.pdf); [von Mayrhauser and Vans 1995](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs69995-PC/papers/von_mayrhauser-1995.pdf)
- Von Mayrhauser and Vans (1995): the survey's own table of experiments shows "only one experiment investigating cognition elements used large-scale code" and two used medium-sized code (they define large as over 40,000 lines); most experiments were observational; "Theories regarding large-scale program comprehension for specialized maintenance tasks are in their infancy." The round 1 quotation is now verified. Type: survey. Population: mixed. Strength: Moderate as description; Very weak as a basis for training. `[FULL]` — [von Mayrhauser and Vans 1995](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs69995-PC/papers/von_mayrhauser-1995.pdf)

### Inferences
- The generation effect all but vanishes for unfamiliar material (nonwords 0.05) and is about half as large in between-subjects designs (0.28). Predicting about a part of the system the engineer does not know may give little of the memory benefit; the benefit depends on having something to generate from. This matches the Sinha and Kapur explanation that attempt-first fails when the learner cannot tell what in the attempt was wrong.
- The feedback meta-analysis gives the clearest design pointer among the newly read sources: a bare "right/wrong" or score sits near 0.24 to 0.46, while feedback that explains why and what to do sits near 0.99 (students, cognitive outcomes). A diagram that only shows what happened is corrective feedback; pairing it with why the prediction missed is the high-information form.
- Tetzlaff's asymmetry cuts against a simple reading of expertise reversal. Removing guidance from the knowledgeable gains less (0.43) than giving it to novices gains (0.51), and the authors advise giving assistance when unsure. For a tool used by experienced engineers on parts of a system they may not know, optional and collapsible explanation fits the evidence better than no explanation.
- Productive failure showed nothing for procedural knowledge and a reversal for domain-general skills. If the goal is "better general judgment about systems", this literature gives no support; if it is "understanding of this concept in this system", it gives moderate support in students.
- The comprehension classics studied 15-line to a-few-hundred-line programs. They support the idea that readers form hypotheses and go wrong on scattered code; they say nothing about architecture-level models.

### Gaps
- Fiorella and Mayer (2016), "Eight ways to promote generative learning": not retrieved. Springer serves a browser challenge, and the book version is lending-only at the Internet Archive. Their per-strategy median effect sizes are not quoted here.
- Letovsky (1987), journal version: not retrieved (closed access; no open copy found).
- Schmeck et al. (2014): abstract-level only, through secondary summaries.
- Pennington's separate 1987 paper on comprehension strategies (the "cross-referencing" finding often attributed to her) was not retrieved; the report I read calls the situation-model account speculative.
- Mellers et al. 2014: I did not extract a percentage Brier improvement; Table 1 values were read but the row labels did not survive text extraction cleanly, so I do not quote them.

## 2. Topics not searched before: explaining to others, feedback timing, scoring rules, calibration training in professionals, durability

### Takeaway
Feedback timing makes no reliable difference on average (g = 0.03), so a delay of minutes while an agent runs is unlikely to matter. Calibration training reliably makes people less confident, which fixes overconfidence but is not the same as better judgment, and in professionals the results are mixed: forecasters held gains for a year with continuing feedback, intelligence analysts shifted confidence without better discrimination, oncologists did not improve. I found no human experiment showing that Brier or log scoring teaches better.

### Cited Findings

**Explaining to others and teaching expectancy**
- Kobayashi (2019), meta-analysis of 28 studies: preparing to teach g = 0.35; teaching after preparing g = 0.56, larger when the teaching was interactive; effects reported to hold after a delay. Kobayashi (2024), 39 studies: teaching after studying versus studying only g = 0.27 (95% CI 0.15 to 0.39); about 0.48 when learners studied expecting to teach, about −0.02 when they did not. Type: meta-analyses. Population: students. STUDENT-RECALL (learning tests). Strength: Weak for this tool. `[SECONDARY]` (search summaries of the abstracts; publisher pages blocked) — [Kobayashi 2019 record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2886178087); [Kobayashi 2024 record](https://x-mol.com/paper/1763030854519197696)
- In the Good Judgment Project, team forecasters were explicitly taught to explain forecasts to others and critique each other, and teams beat independent forecasters (t(1120) = 5.92, p < .001; I did not confirm which year this test refers to). The design does not separate the effect of explaining from the effect of seeing others' information. Type: randomized field experiment. Population: educated adult volunteers. Strength: Weak for explaining as such. `[FULL]` — [Mellers et al. 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4rg4n9vr/qt4rg4n9vr.pdf)
- Chi and Wylie's framework places explaining to a partner who responds ("interactive") above self-explaining ("constructive"); this is a prediction supported by student studies, not a pooled estimate. `[FULL]` — [Chi and Wylie 2014](https://chilab.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf)

**Immediate versus delayed feedback**
- Kandemir, Esposito, Gurgand and Ramus (2026), Educational Psychology Review: 51 studies, 160 effect sizes, computer-assisted learning, 1988 to 2024. Delays ranged from 1 second to 7 days, or 1 to 60 intervening items. Overall g = 0.03 (95% CI −0.08 to 0.13, p = .61): no reliable difference. Long-delay subset g = −0.06 (p = .52). Task complexity did not moderate the effect (lower-order versus higher-order, g = −0.10, p = .24), nor did prior knowledge adjustment. Text memorisation favoured delayed feedback (g = −0.30, p < .001); cognitive skills, language and STEM showed no significant advantage either way. Primary and secondary pupils did relatively better with delayed feedback than tertiary and adult learners (g = −0.23, p = .04). Only 6 effect sizes came from adult education, and few studies used delays of a day or more. Type: meta-analysis. Population: mostly students. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Moderate that timing within minutes to hours is not decisive; Weak for professionals and for delays of weeks. `[FULL]` — [Kandemir et al. 2026, author copy](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR26.pdf)
- The same paper notes the previous direct meta-analysis was Kulik and Kulik (1988), and that earlier reviews (Shute 2008; Van der Kleij et al. 2015) had suggested immediate feedback for lower-order and delayed for higher-order tasks; the new analysis did not confirm that split. `[FULL]` — [same source](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR26.pdf)
- Software maintenance: Jørgensen and Sjøberg (2002) argue from the judgment literature (Balzer et al. 1989) that *outcome* feedback alone (predicted versus actual) "does not enable learning from experience"; what helps is feedback on the relations inside the task. See section 3 for the study. `[FULL]` — [Jørgensen and Sjøberg 2002](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/SE.4.Joergensen.2002.a.pdf)

**Which scoring rule**
- In the Good Judgment Project all main effects and interactions "held up with spherical and logarithmic rules"; the paper orders the rules by how hard they punish extreme overconfidence: spherical most lenient, Brier intermediate, logarithmic most punitive. This shows the *findings* did not depend on the rule; it is not a test of which rule trains people better, since forecasters were only ever shown Brier scores. Type: robustness analysis. `[FULL]` — [Mellers et al. 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4rg4n9vr/qt4rg4n9vr.pdf)
- Greenberg (2018, revised 2020) argues, without an experiment, that for a calibration *training* product properness is neither sufficient nor always necessary: most proper rules are unintuitive to users, and he proposes "practical" rules built around an intuitive right-versus-wrong reading. Type: methods paper and opinion. Strength: Very weak as evidence. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Greenberg, arXiv 1808.07501](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.07501)

**Calibration training with feedback in professionals**
- Forecasters, durability: Moore et al. (author manuscript of the Management Science 2017 paper). Three years of forecasts. One hour of probability training, which included a knowledge test with confidence ratings and accuracy feedback, before any forecasting; forecasters then received regular feedback. Overconfidence (confidence minus hit rate) by condition (Table 1): working alone, no training 4.3%; alone with training 2.0%; team, no training 2.8%; team with training 0.6%. The authors write that "an hour of training halves overconfidence over the following year" and that training "mostly improves calibration by reducing confidence". Calibration did **not** improve with more years of forecasting experience (no significant difference between forecasters with one, two or three years). Self-rated expertise was not a reliable moderator of accuracy. Type: randomized conditions in a field tournament. Population: educated adult volunteers. Strength: Moderate to Strong that brief training plus continuing feedback holds overconfidence down for a year. `[FULL]` (manuscript version; published numbers may differ slightly, and the manuscript's abstract and body give 3% and 2.1% for overall overconfidence) — [Moore et al., author manuscript](http://learnmoore.org/CAC/CAC.pdf)
- Intelligence analysts: Kelly and Mandel (2023 preprint). 70 Canadian intelligence analysts, before and after a commercial self-paced online calibration course of 3 to 4 hours with calibration tests and feedback. Calibration improved overall but depended on the task: on interval estimates analysts were overconfident before and better calibrated after; on binary choices they were underconfident before and became *more* underconfident after. Improvement on the two tasks was uncorrelated. The authors conclude the training "shifted analyst bias toward less confidence rather than improve metacognitive monitoring ability". Type: pre-post study, preprint, no randomized control described in the abstract. Population: working intelligence analysts. Strength: Moderate (right population; weak design). `[FULL]` (abstract and introduction read; I found no delayed follow-up in the text) — [Kelly and Mandel preprint](https://osf.io/download/p82nx/)
- Oncologists: Doussau et al. (2024, Clinical Trials). 148 cancer specialists randomized to a feedback training module (predict five completed trials, get feedback on accuracy and confidence) or none, then 1,217 forecasts on 25 ongoing trials. No improvement: mean Brier 0.22 (control) versus 0.21 (feedback), p = 0.51. Type: RCT. Population: practising specialist physicians. Strength: Moderate that a single short feedback module does not improve expert prediction. `[FULL]` — [Doussau et al. 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11005298/)
- Medical interns: Staal et al. (2024). 125 Dutch interns randomized to no feedback, right/wrong feedback, or feedback with an explanation, on 20 chest X-rays; then 10 more with no feedback, immediately after. Calibration (variance in accuracy explained by confidence) rose from R² = 0.05 with no feedback to 0.12 with right/wrong feedback and 0.19 with explained feedback. The authors say it is unclear whether this reflects better confidence estimates or simply better accuracy. Type: RCT, immediate test only. Population: final-phase medical students. Strength: Weak to Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` (PMC full text fetched; abstract read) — [Staal et al. 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927782/)
- Weather forecasters: Murphy and Winkler (1977) analysed operational US National Weather Service precipitation probability forecasts and concluded that forecasters "can and do formulate reliable probability forecasts"; reliability of the precipitation forecasts over a four-year sample was very high. This is evidence that calibration is achievable under daily, unambiguous outcome feedback; it is not a training experiment. Type: observational analysis of operational forecasts. Population: professional forecasters. Strength: Moderate as an existence proof. `[FULL]` (scanned copy; abstract and results passages read) — [Murphy and Winkler 1977](http://nwafiles.nwas.org/digest/papers/1977/Vol02No2/1977v002no02-MurphyWinkler.pdf)
- Software maintainers reward overconfidence socially: in Jørgensen and Sjøberg's follow-up work, "maintainers who provide high confidence predictions were interpreted by other software professionals as more knowledgeable ... even in situations were the low confidence predictions were clearly more accurate". Reported as a preliminary result. `[FULL]` — [Jørgensen and Sjøberg 2002](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/SE.4.Joergensen.2002.a.pdf)
- Hindsight bias: a meta-analysis of 95 studies reports a mean effect of d = 0.39 (Guilbault et al. 2004); telling people about the bias does not remove it. This is the standard argument for recording the prediction before the outcome is shown. `[SECONDARY]` (search summary) — [summary page](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/hindsight-bias/)

### Inferences
- The delay while an agent works (minutes) sits well inside the range where the meta-analysis finds no timing effect. Timing is not the thing to optimise; the informational content of the feedback is (section 1, Wisniewski).
- Across forecasters, analysts and interns, calibration feedback moves people toward lower confidence. That helps where people start overconfident and hurts where they start underconfident (analysts on binary choices). A tool should show an engineer their own calibration curve rather than push confidence down across the board, and should expect that better calibration will not by itself mean better discrimination between safe and risky changes.
- Durability evidence exists only where feedback continued (forecasters, weather forecasters). No study I found tested whether calibration gains persist after feedback stops.
- More years of forecasting did not improve calibration (Moore), and more years of maintenance did not improve prediction (Jørgensen, section 3). Experience without structured feedback does not calibrate.
- On scoring rules the honest position is: any proper rule is defensible; the logarithmic rule punishes a confident miss hardest, which may be discouraging for a voluntary tool; there is no human evidence that either rule teaches better. Given Wisniewski, how the score is explained probably matters more than which rule produces it. This is inference.

### Gaps
- Kobayashi's meta-analyses were not read in the source. I found no study of explaining-to-others or teaching expectancy in working professionals.
- Lichtenstein and Fischhoff (1980), the classic calibration-training study, was again not retrieved; Mellers et al. cite it as showing brief training paying off over time.
- No controlled human study found comparing Brier and logarithmic scoring for learning or engagement.
- No study found on calibration training for software engineers.
- No evidence found on calibration durability after feedback is withdrawn.
- Feedback timing for judgment (as opposed to learning items) in professionals: the meta-analysis had 6 adult-education effect sizes; nothing specific to professional judgment tasks.

## 3. Direct evidence: do developers predict change impact well, can it be trained, and how accurate are their models of the system?

### Takeaway
There is real, if old and thin, direct evidence. Professionals asked to predict which parts of a system a change will touch are mostly right about what they name but miss a good share of what actually changes, and are unaware of it. Maintenance experience does not improve prediction of problems, and outcome feedback alone improved only a few developers. No study uses change-impact prediction as a training intervention, and none tracks it over months.

### Cited Findings

**Predicting the reach of a change**
- Lindvall and Sandahl (1998), "How well do experienced software developers predict software change?": a four-year industrial case study of an object-oriented C++ system (the PMR project) in which developers, as normal work, predicted from the requirements of a release which classes would change, over two releases. Reported by a later paper as average recall 0.76 and precision 0.96. Search summaries of the abstract say correctness of what was predicted was high, under-prediction was the problem ("many more classes than predicted are changed"), and "project developers are unaware of their own positive and negative capabilities in predicting change". Type: longitudinal industrial case study. Population: experienced professional developers, one project. Strength: Moderate (right population and task; one project; numbers second-hand). `[SECONDARY]` — figures from [Falessi et al., arXiv 1808.06359, section 7.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.06359) (`[FULL]` for that passage); abstract wording from search summaries of [the journal record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0164121298100195)
- Tao, Dang, Xie, Zhang and Kim (FSE 2012), Microsoft: 180 survey participants. Understanding code changes is needed "typically several times each day". Engineers want to know a change's quality (correctness, completeness, consistency), its **risk ("does this change break any code elsewhere?")**, and most of all its rationale; "engineers face non-trivial challenges when determining the risk of a change", relying on testing and code review. Participants asked for tools that decompose composite changes. Type: survey plus interviews. Population: professional engineers, one company. Strength: Moderate (descriptive). `[FULL]` (abstract and introduction read) — [Tao et al. 2012, Microsoft Research copy](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/howdosoftwareengineersunderstandcodechanges_fse2012.pdf)

**Does experience or feedback improve prediction?**
- Jørgensen and Sjøberg (2002), "Impact of experience on maintenance skills": 54 maintainers in one Norwegian company, one maintenance task each; before starting, each predicted whether the task would meet major unexpected problems. One third of tasks did. Findings: (1) more years of general maintenance experience did not reduce unexpected problems beyond a moderate level, but more experience *with the specific application* did; (2) "The most experienced maintainers did not predict maintenance problems better than maintainers with little or medium experience"; (3) a simple one-variable model beat the maintainers' predictions. The authors attribute this to a lack of meaningful feedback, to prediction by analogy with a few remembered tasks, and to no probabilistic thinking. They add that "much of the experience from maintaining one application seems difficult to transfer to another application". Type: field study with statistical tests; small n, low power acknowledged. Population: professional maintainers. Strength: Moderate. `[FULL]` — [Jørgensen and Sjøberg 2002, Simula copy](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/SE.4.Joergensen.2002.a.pdf)
- Same paper, preliminary follow-up results where professionals predicted effort and risk and got accuracy feedback right after each prediction: "While most developers do not improve their risk predictions when provided with prediction accuracy feedback, a few of them improve the risk predictions very soon"; developers left very unlikely problems out of their estimates even after seeing them occur often. Type: preliminary report, no numbers. Strength: Weak. `[FULL]` — [same source](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/SE.4.Joergensen.2002.a.pdf)
- Developers judging machine output: a 2026 controlled experiment with 86 Python programmers judging whether LLM-generated assertions matched a function's behaviour. Accuracy 74% on correct assertions, **49% on incorrect ones** (p < .001, odds ratio 2.94), with similar high confidence in both. Low-quality natural-language explanations lowered accuracy (odds ratio 0.58, p = .037) while raising confidence (p = .005); high-quality explanations did not improve accuracy. Type: controlled experiment plus think-aloud follow-up; preprint. Population: Python programmers (mix not checked). Strength: Moderate for "a plausible but wrong artefact misleads and inflates confidence". `[FULL]` (abstract and introduction read) — [arXiv 2607.08885](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.08885)

**Accuracy of engineers' models of their own architecture**
- Rosik, Le Gear, Buckley, Babar and Connolly (2011), longitudinal in-vivo case study on a commercial system using reflexion modelling (compare the intended architecture with the code): the system diverged from the intended architecture during initial implementation; the method concealed some real violations; finding inconsistencies did not lead the small team to remove them. Type: industrial case study. Population: professionals, one system. Strength: Moderate that intended and built architecture diverge early; no measure of individual accuracy. `[SECONDARY]` (search summary of repository abstracts) — [IBM Research record](https://research.ibm.com/publications/assessing-architectural-drift-in-commercial-software-development-a-case-study)
- Anthony, Berntsson, Santilli and Wohlrab (ICSA 2024): 11 interviews and 63 survey responses on architectural drift. Juniors lean on documentation, seniors on experience; developers commonly make ad hoc decisions about how components connect; respondents want the *reasons* behind decisions documented, which currently is rare. Type: mixed-methods, self-report. Population: professional developers. Strength: Weak to Moderate (descriptive). `[FULL]` (abstract and introduction read) — [Anthony et al. 2024](https://rebekkaa.github.io/files/2024_ICSA.pdf)
- A Chalmers industrial case study compared the implemented architecture (reverse-engineered) with the architecture as developers described it in interviews and found gaps such as missing modules and missing communication paths. Type: case study (thesis). Strength: Weak. `[SECONDARY]` — [Chalmers record](https://odr.chalmers.se/items/448bf3a5-3590-43e0-abe4-546e96ac8502)
- Automatic recovery of architecture from code is itself inaccurate: Garcia, Ivkovic and Medvidovic (ASE 2013) compared six recovery techniques against eight verified ground-truth architectures and report that "even the best of the lot has surprisingly low accuracy"; Lutellier et al. (ICSE 2015) found accuracy "low for all recovery techniques" even with better dependency inputs. Type: tool evaluations. Strength: Moderate that an automatically derived architecture view cannot be assumed correct. `[SECONDARY]` (search summaries of abstracts) — [ASE 2013 record](https://search.isc.ac/inventory/49/660580.htm); [ICSE 2015 record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c285t-d1ec05c5ca17c555875fbb7ba0041d3a47b40a35356f399c8cc4f79ec959f0d13)

**Prediction as teaching in computing, beyond tracing**
- Tucker (UCLA dissertation 2022; later published in Learning and Instruction as "Prediction versus production for teaching computer programming"): 166 psychology undergraduates signed up; random assignment to predict the output of provided code versus modify or write code. The predict group showed "more positive emotional trajectories, increased motivation, and greater learning". The predictions were line-level (for example the output of a print statement) for people with no programming background. Type: randomized experiment. Population: novice undergraduates. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Weak for this tool. `[ABSTRACT]` (dissertation full text fetched; abstract and method read; effect sizes not extracted) — [Tucker 2022, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8zp2v3bd/qt8zp2v3bd.pdf)
- I searched for predict-observe-explain or prediction-before-outcome studies at the level of distributed systems, networking or architecture and found none.

**Onboarding**
- Search summaries point to two small Microsoft field studies (eight new graduates observed for 85 hours over their first six months; eight projects with repository data and four interviews) and a study of globally distributed projects in which newcomers did not reach the productivity target in the observed period. None measured the accuracy of a newcomer's model of the architecture. `[SECONDARY]` — [systematic review of onboarding tools, arXiv 2408.15989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.15989)

### Inferences
- Lindvall and Sandahl, and Jørgensen and Sjøberg, together are the closest thing to a baseline for this tool: experienced developers name the right things and miss others (high precision, lower recall), do not know how good they are, and do not get better with years. That is the profile for which recorded predictions with feedback are usually prescribed, and it suggests the tool's most useful output is the list of *what the developer did not predict*.
- Jørgensen's reading of the judgment literature warns that comparing predicted with actual is not enough; learning needs feedback about relations inside the task. Applied here: the picture should show *why* the unpredicted part was reached (the dependency path), not only that it was.
- Application-specific experience helped and did not carry across applications. Expect the tool to build knowledge of this system, not a portable skill (see section 4).
- Two findings bear on trusting the tool's own output: automatic architecture recovery is inaccurate, and developers accept wrong machine-made artefacts about half the time with full confidence. A wrong diagram would be taken as feedback and teach the wrong lesson. This is the largest risk the evidence points to.
- Tao et al. confirm that "what could this break elsewhere" and "why was this done" are the two hardest things about a change for professionals, which supports the choice of prediction target.

### Gaps
- Lindvall and Sandahl (1998) full text not retrieved (closed access; the open thesis copy that summarises it would not download). Recall 0.76 and precision 0.96 are from a citing paper.
- No study in any population uses prediction of software change impact as a learning intervention with a control group.
- No study measures an individual engineer's architecture model against the real system with a score, and none tracks it over time.
- No predict-observe-explain study found for systems-level computing topics.
- No onboarding study found that measures understanding rather than activity or self-report.
- Jørgensen's follow-up feedback studies were reported as preliminary; I did not search for the later published versions.

## 4. Transfer: will months of this practice carry to new systems or to general judgment?

### Takeaway
The best evidence says no for far transfer and yes for near transfer. Training improves what is trained and close neighbours; claims of broad carry-over shrink to zero in well-controlled studies. In software specifically, experience with one application did not carry to another.

### Cited Findings
- Sala and Gobet (2017), Current Directions in Psychological Science, summarising their three meta-analyses of chess, music and working-memory training in children: overall effects 0.34, 0.16 and 0.12, but "the size of the effects was inversely related to the quality of the experimental design"; with active control groups the far-transfer effects were "minimal or null". The exception was near transfer: working-memory training improved other memory tasks by 0.44 to 0.46 SD against active and passive controls. Conclusion: training "does not reliably enhance any skill beyond the skills they train". Type: summary of meta-analyses. Population: children and young adolescents. Strength: Moderate (different population and skill type, but a consistent pattern). `[FULL]` — [Sala and Gobet 2017, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724589/)
- Sala et al. (2019), second-order meta-analysis in Collabra: near transfer of working-memory training is real and moderated by population; far transfer is small or null, and after correcting for placebo effects and publication bias the far-transfer effect and its true variance are both reported as zero. `[SECONDARY]` (search summary of the abstract) — [Liverpool repository record](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3043493/)
- Barnett and Ceci (2002): a taxonomy with nine dimensions (three of content, six of context) on which a transfer episode can be near or far; the abstract argues a single verdict on "far transfer" is misguided because studies differ on these dimensions. Not read. `[SECONDARY]` — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12081085/)
- Software: "much of the experience from maintaining one application seems difficult to transfer to another application"; application-specific experience reduced unexpected problems where general experience did not. Type: field study. Population: professional maintainers. Strength: Moderate. `[FULL]` — [Jørgensen and Sjøberg 2002](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/SE.4.Joergensen.2002.a.pdf)
- Productive failure reversed for domain-general skills (g = −0.17). `[FULL]` — [Sinha and Kapur 2021](https://web.archive.org/web/2id_/https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- Calibration training in analysts: improvement on interval estimation and on binary choice was uncorrelated, i.e. even within calibration the gain did not carry across task formats. `[FULL]` — [Kelly and Mandel preprint](https://osf.io/download/p82nx/)
- Workplace training: Grossman and Salas (2011) review the "transfer problem", citing the long-quoted but poorly sourced estimate that only about 10% of training spending transfers to the job, and identify trainee motivation, a supportive environment and opportunity to perform as the factors with the strongest support. Blume et al. (2010, 89 studies) is the main meta-analysis; I saw it only in summaries. `[FULL]` for Grossman and Salas (skimmed); `[SECONDARY]` for Blume — [Grossman and Salas 2011](https://ftq.qc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2019/04/Grossman_et_al-2011-International_Journal_of_Training_and_Development.pdf)
- For transfer (positive side): in forecasting, probability training given once improved accuracy across 199 varied geopolitical questions over two years. That is transfer across questions inside one task format (probability forecasts of discrete events). `[FULL]` — [Mellers et al. 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4rg4n9vr/qt4rg4n9vr.pdf)

### Inferences
- On Barnett and Ceci's logic, predicting the effect of the next change to the same system is very near transfer (same knowledge domain, same context, same format), and the evidence supports gains there. Predicting effects in a new system is far on the knowledge dimension, and the software evidence (Jørgensen) is against it.
- What may carry across systems is the *habit and method* (list what could be reached, check base rates, state confidence), by analogy with forecasting training, which taught a method and transferred across questions. That is an analogy, not a finding for software.
- The tool should not claim to build a general "systems thinking" ability. The supportable claim is narrower: better knowledge of this system and better-calibrated confidence about changes to it.

### Gaps
- Barnett and Ceci (2002) and Sala et al. (2019) were not read in full.
- No study found on transfer of system-understanding or architecture-judgment training to a new codebase.
- The far-transfer meta-analyses concern cognitive training in children; no equivalent synthesis found for professional judgment training.

## 5. Long-run professional learning inside work, beyond Kerfoot

### Takeaway
Feeding professionals data on their own performance changes practice by a small amount on average (median 2.7 percentage points, mean 6.2), with a decline in about a quarter of trials, and works better when it is individual, compared against top performers, delivered by someone known, interactive, and paired with a concrete plan. Repeating it more often was associated with *smaller* effects. Reminders alone did not raise engagement with spaced cases over 16 months.

### Cited Findings
- Ivers et al. (2025), Cochrane review of audit and feedback (update of the 2012 review; search to June 2020): 177 studies with 558 dichotomous practice outcomes versus control. Median absolute improvement in desired practice 2.7% (interquartile range 0.0 to 8.6); meta-analytic mean 6.2% (95% CI 4.1 to 8.2; odds ratio 1.47, 95% CI 1.31 to 1.64; moderate certainty). **A decline in performance occurred in 27% of studies.** Larger effects with lower baseline performance and with more co-interventions. Type: Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials. Population: practising health professionals. Strength: Strong for the size and variability of feedback effects on professional behaviour; Moderate as analogy for engineers. `[FULL]` (abstract, summary of findings and results sections read) — [Ivers et al. 2025, UCL open copy](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10207244/1/Ivers_et_al-2025-Cochrane_Database_of_Systematic_Reviews.pdf)
- Same review, what made feedback work better (meta-regression): individual-level rather than team-level data; comparison with top peers or a benchmark rather than the average; a local champion with an existing relationship to the recipient; interactive rather than only written or didactic delivery (multiple formats, ratio of odds ratios 1.33); facilitation to engage with the feedback (1.11); specific action plans given (1.16). No significant effect of the number of indicators, of comparison to the average, or of co-developing action plans (1.06, CI crosses 1). "Contrary to expectations, repeated delivery was associated with lower effect size." `[FULL]` — [same source](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10207244/1/Ivers_et_al-2025-Cochrane_Database_of_Systematic_Reviews.pdf)
- Same review on decay: outcomes were taken at the point closest to the end of the intervention; the authors state sustainability "is of great interest, but beyond" the review's scope. So the best synthesis of professional feedback has no estimate of decay. `[FULL]` — [same source](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10207244/1/Ivers_et_al-2025-Cochrane_Database_of_Systematic_Reviews.pdf)
- Grad et al. (2021), cluster randomized trial in 12 Canadian family medicine residency programmes: an app version that sent weekly alerts to a "case of the week" for about 16 months, versus the same app without alerts. No difference between groups in number of cases completed, and no significant effect on certification exam scores (522 residents linked; adjusted difference +0.6 points, CI crossing zero). Type: cluster RCT. Population: resident physicians. Strength: Moderate that reminders alone do not produce sustained voluntary engagement or learning. `[FULL]` (abstract, methods and results read) — [Grad et al. 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8338813/)
- Deliberate practice in simulation-based medical education: McGaghie et al. (2011) report an overall effect size of 0.71 (95% CI 0.65 to 0.76) across 14 studies comparing simulation with deliberate practice against traditional clinical education, for specific clinical skills. The 0.71 is a correlation-type effect size in that paper, the sample of studies is small, and the outcomes are procedural skills in trainees. Type: meta-analysis. Population: medical trainees. Strength: Weak for this tool. `[SECONDARY]` (search summary of the abstract; PMC full text failed to load) — [McGaghie et al. 2011, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/3102783/)
- Checklists: Haynes et al. (2009) reported in-hospital deaths falling from 1.5% to 0.8% and complications from 11% to 7% after introducing the WHO surgical checklist in eight hospitals (before-and-after design); Urbach et al. (2014), covering 101 Ontario hospitals after mandated adoption, found no significant reduction in adjusted mortality or complications. Commentators attribute the gap to implementation quality and nominal compliance. Type: before-and-after studies. Population: surgical teams. Strength: Weak for this tool, useful as a caution. `[SECONDARY]` — [commentary with both results](https://www.patientsafetysolutions.com/docs/April_2014_Checklists_Dont_Always_Lead_to_Improvement.htm)
- Software analogue of "experience is not practice": 54 maintainers showed no improvement in prediction with years, absent structured feedback. `[FULL]` — [Jørgensen and Sjøberg 2002](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/SE.4.Joergensen.2002.a.pdf)

### Inferences
- The realistic effect to expect from showing professionals their own performance is a few percentage points on a behaviour, with wide spread and a real chance of no effect or harm. The Kerfoot result in round 1 (a 40% relative reduction) is at the favourable end, on one simple rule.
- The features that raised effects in the Cochrane review map onto design choices: per-person feedback, a benchmark worth aiming at rather than the team average, a concrete next step, and a human channel (a known colleague) alongside the tool. A purely private, purely written feedback loop is the weaker form.
- "Repeated delivery associated with lower effect" agrees with round 1's habituation evidence and with Kerfoot's plateau after cycle 2. Frequency should be kept low.
- The contrast between Haynes and Urbach is a warning about mandating the tool: adoption that is nominal produces nothing.
- Grad et al. shows that prompts do not by themselves create engagement over 16 months; see section 6.

### Gaps
- No pooled estimate of how fast feedback effects decay after the feedback stops.
- McGaghie et al. and the checklist studies were not read in the source.
- No months-long controlled study found of a practice-with-feedback intervention in software engineers.
- Deliberate practice evidence in surgery concerns motor and procedural skills on simulators; I found nothing comparable for diagnostic or system-level judgment in engineering.

## 6. Motivation and voluntary use over months

### Takeaway
I found no study of what keeps professionals using an optional, effortful learning tool for months. The nearby evidence says: habits take about two to five months to form and vary enormously between people; reminders alone do not sustain use; streak displays help while intact and demotivate once broken; rewards and bare scores hurt motivation more often than informative feedback does; and developers drop tools that interrupt them or are wrong too often.

### Cited Findings
- Habit formation: a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of health-behaviour habit studies (20 studies, 2,601 participants). Median time to reach habit thresholds 59 to 66 days; means 106 to 154 days; individual range 4 to 335 days. In one study only 23% of participants reached the habit threshold. Habit-strength scores rose from before to after intervention (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.88; 12 studies). Morning practice formed habits faster than evening in one study. The review rejects the "21 days" idea. Type: systematic review and meta-analysis; six of 20 studies single-group. Population: adults, health behaviours (diet, exercise, flossing). Strength: Weak for this tool (simple daily behaviours, self-report). `[FULL]` — [Singh et al. 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/)
- Self-determination theory at work: a 2026 meta-analysis (192 studies) confirms the pattern that perceived support from leaders for autonomy, competence and relatedness is linked to need satisfaction, then to autonomous motivation, then to better workplace outcomes, with substantial variation between studies. The data are correlations from surveys. Type: meta-analysis of correlational studies. Population: employees across sectors. Strength: Weak as a guide to design (correlational, general workplace outcomes, not learning-tool use). `[ABSTRACT]` (PMC full text fetched; abstract and aims read) — [Stress and Health 2026, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12887546/)
- Feedback and motivation: in the Wisniewski meta-analysis feedback had a smaller effect on motivational outcomes (d = 0.33) than cognitive ones; 21% of motivational effects were negative, and 86% of the interventions behind those were uninformative rewards or punishments. The authors' explanation follows self-determination theory: controlling, uninformative feedback reduces felt autonomy and competence. Population: students. `[FULL]` — [Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/pdf)
- Streaks: Silverman and Barasch (Journal of Consumer Research 2023, seven studies) report that showing an intact streak increases the behaviour and showing a broken one reduces it (in one study 45.21% continued when the broken streak was highlighted versus 60.90% when it was not); the option to repair a streak softens the drop. A 2024 randomized trial on a school mathematics platform with about 60,000 pupils is reported to have found highlighting streaks raised logins by 2.8 percentage points with no discouragement effect. Neither involves professionals or learning outcomes. Type: experiments. Population: consumers; school pupils. Strength: Weak. `[SECONDARY]` (search summaries) — [Silverman and Barasch record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361658509_On_or_Off_Track_How_Broken_Streaks_Affect_Consumer_Decisions); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- Gamification misuse: a qualitative study of Duolingo (nine years of forum posts plus 15 interviews) describes users becoming "too fixated on gamification" and distracted from learning, with effects on learning and well-being; common drivers named are competitiveness, overindulgence in playfulness and herding. No peer-reviewed evaluation of Duolingo's streak on learning was found. Type: qualitative. Population: adult language learners. Strength: Weak. `[ABSTRACT]` — [arXiv 2203.16175](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.16175)
- Reminders: weekly alerts for 16 months produced no more case completion than no alerts in resident physicians. `[FULL]` — [Grad et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8338813/)
- Attrition as the norm: Eysenbach's "law of attrition" (2005) argues that in voluntary internet interventions a large share of users stop using them, that this should be measured and modelled, and gives examples of trials where few users completed all modules. Type: viewpoint. Strength: Weak. `[ABSTRACT]` (opening sections read) — [Eysenbach 2005, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1550631/)
- What sustained forecasters: in the Good Judgment Project attrition was 7% in Year 1 and 3% in Year 2, with payment ($150 then $250 for meeting a minimum of 25 questions a year, regardless of accuracy), leaderboards showing the top 10%, and teams. Participants answered far more than the minimum (48 and 65 questions on average). Paid, self-selected volunteers. `[FULL]` — [Mellers et al. 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4rg4n9vr/qt4rg4n9vr.pdf)
- Why developers stop using tools: Johnson et al. (ICSE 2013) interviewed 20 developers about static analysis tools; all saw value, but false positives and the way warnings are presented were barriers to use. `[ABSTRACT]` (search summary of abstract) — [ICSE 2013 page](https://2013.icse-conferences.org/content/why-dont-software-developers-use-static-analysis-tools-find-bugs.html)
- Developer experience under AI assistance: Vella and Blincoe (2026 preprint), two surveys six months apart, 158 then 101 professional engineers (95 matched). 82% reported less time writing code; work shifted from creating to verifying ("supervisory engineering work"); productivity perceptions stayed positive while developer experience eroded. Self-report. Type: longitudinal survey. Population: professional engineers. Strength: Weak to Moderate (descriptive). `[ABSTRACT]` — [arXiv 2605.23135](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23135)
- Social pressure against honest confidence: maintainers who state high confidence are seen as more knowledgeable even when wrong (preliminary). `[FULL]` — [Jørgensen and Sjøberg 2002](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/SE.4.Joergensen.2002.a.pdf)

### Inferences
- Nothing here licenses a confident retention design. The safest reading: do not rely on reminders or streaks; tie the prompt to a moment the engineer already chose (starting an agent run); keep it rare; make the feedback informative enough to be worth the effort on its own.
- A streak mechanic carries a known failure mode (the broken streak) and a known distortion (optimising the counter). For a tool whose prompts depend on when real changes happen, streaks would also break for reasons outside the user's control.
- If predictions or confidence ratings are visible to colleagues, the social reward for sounding sure (Jørgensen) works against calibration. Private scoring, or team norms that reward calibration, would be needed. Against that, the Cochrane review found feedback worked better with a known person involved. These pull in different directions and the evidence does not settle it.
- Engineers whose work is shifting toward verifying AI output (Vella and Blincoe) may experience a prediction prompt as one more supervisory chore. Whether it instead restores a sense of competence is an open, testable question.
- Tool accuracy is a retention issue as well as a learning issue: developers drop tools that are wrong too often.

### Gaps
- No controlled or longitudinal study found of voluntary use of an effortful learning tool by working professionals over months, in any field.
- No self-determination theory *intervention* study in workplace learning tools was found; the meta-analysis is correlational.
- Silverman and Barasch and the school-platform trial were not read in the source.
- No retention or churn data found for developer learning tools; evidence on why developers abandon tools is interview-based.
- Habit formation evidence is for simple daily health behaviours cued by a fixed context, which a change-triggered prompt is not.

## 7. Diagrams: C4, change-level views, software cities, auto-generated views, and wrong diagrams

### Takeaway
I found no controlled evaluation of C4 and none of a diff-level or change-level diagram. Software-city visualisations have several controlled experiments showing better correctness on comprehension tasks than an IDE, mostly with mixed or student samples. The evidence on wrong or machine-generated artefacts is the most decision-relevant: developers spot an incorrect generated artefact only about half the time while staying confident, and poor explanations raise confidence while lowering accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- C4: a targeted search returned only the model's own site, vendor and practitioner pages. No controlled study of C4's effect on comprehension or task performance was found. `[SECONDARY]` (negative search result) — [c4model.com](https://c4model.com/)
- Software cities: Romano, Capece, Erra, Scanniello and Lanza (2019) ran a controlled experiment comparing an IDE (Eclipse with a metrics plugin) with a city-metaphor tool on a normal screen and in immersive virtual reality. Both city versions "significantly improved the correctness of the solutions to program comprehension tasks with respect to Eclipse"; the virtual-reality version was significantly faster than the on-screen city. A search summary gives 42 participants. Type: controlled experiment. Population: not verified here (the abstract does not say; such studies usually use students). Strength: Moderate for purpose-built whole-system views on comprehension tasks; Weak for change diagrams. `[ABSTRACT]` (accepted manuscript fetched; abstract read) — [Romano et al. 2019, accepted manuscript](https://iris.unibas.it/retrieve/2a6c8660-3605-4dea-82f4-55cd414f7750/On%20the%20use%20of%20the%20virtual.pdf)
- Trace visualisation in a software city (DynaCity): a controlled experiment is reported in which professional developers solved comprehension tasks 11.7% more correctly and used 5.83% less of the allotted time than with a traditional trace view in the same city. `[SECONDARY]` (search summary) — [publication record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361538812_Trace_visualization_within_the_Software_City_metaphor_Controlled_experiments_on_program_comprehension)
- Change-level visualisation in code review: Krause-Glau and colleagues (2024) describe a software-city view that shows the static and dynamic differences between two commits inside a code review; the paper presents the approach and implementation and does not report a user evaluation in the text I read. Type: tool paper. Strength: Very weak as evidence. `[FULL]` (skimmed for an evaluation; none found) — [arXiv 2408.08141](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.08141)
- A 2026 survey of code-review benchmarks is reported to note that an execution-trace-augmented diff visualisation was published with no quantitative evaluation. `[SECONDARY]` — [arXiv 2602.13377](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.13377)
- Spatial layout of code: Bouraffa, Fuhrmann and Maalej (2023), online between-subjects experiment with 20 developers comparing a tab-based view with a free-form code canvas. "Neither the spatial representation of code nor the visuo-spatial working memory of developers has a significant impact on comprehension performance"; time spent on navigation and annotation differed. Type: controlled experiment, small n. Population: developers. Strength: Weak (underpowered null). `[ABSTRACT]` — [arXiv 2304.09301](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.09301)
- Wrong machine-made artefacts mislead: 86 Python programmers judged LLM-generated assertions correctly 74% of the time when the assertion was right and 49% when it was wrong, with similar confidence; low-quality explanations reduced accuracy (odds ratio 0.58) and raised confidence. Type: controlled experiment, 2026 preprint. Strength: Moderate. `[FULL]` (abstract and introduction) — [arXiv 2607.08885](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.08885)
- Generated documentation can hinder: Kruse and colleagues (2024), controlled experiment with 20 professional developers and 30 students using LLM-generated code documentation; a search summary of the paper says the generated documentation in some cases supported and in some cases hindered comprehension, and the abstract notes participants rarely assessed the quality of what was generated. Type: controlled experiment. Population: professionals and students. Strength: Weak to Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [arXiv 2408.00686](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.00686)
- Auto-derived architecture is unreliable at source: architecture recovery techniques have "surprisingly low accuracy" against verified ground truth (Garcia et al. 2013; Lutellier et al. 2015). `[SECONDARY]` — [ASE 2013 record](https://search.isc.ac/inventory/49/660580.htm)
- Drawing accuracy matters for learning from one's own drawing: in every study that scored it, the accuracy of the learner's drawing correlated with later performance (Van Meter and Garner). Students. `[FULL]` — [Van Meter and Garner 2005](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-promise-and-practice-of-learner-generated-drawing-ioq0lo8in0.pdf)
- Documentation and drift in practice: documented architecture tends to look less coupled than the implemented one, and reasons for decisions are rarely written down (section 3). `[FULL]` for [Anthony et al. 2024](https://rebekkaa.github.io/files/2024_ICSA.pdf); `[SECONDARY]` for the coupling claim.

### Inferences
- The tool's diagram is an auto-generated artefact about a change. The two relevant findings point the same way: such artefacts are often wrong at the architecture level, and readers do not catch the errors and become more confident when a fluent explanation accompanies them. Since the diagram is the feedback that the prediction is scored against, its correctness is the binding constraint on whether the tool teaches or misteaches.
- A diagram built from facts that can be checked mechanically (files and symbols changed, call or import edges touched, tests run) is on firmer ground than one built from inferred architecture or inferred consequences. Marking which parts are observed and which are inferred follows from the evidence, though no study has tested such marking.
- The positive software-visualisation results come from whole-system views used for exploration tasks, with tools the participants were trained on. They do not show that a small per-change picture improves understanding of a change.
- C4's standing remains practitioner convention. It may be a sensible vocabulary for levels of abstraction, but nothing found supports a claim that it improves comprehension.

### Gaps
- No controlled evaluation found of C4, of diff-level or impact diagrams, or of LLM-generated architecture or change diagrams on human comprehension or review quality.
- No study found that measures the harm from an inaccurate or stale *diagram* specifically; the evidence is from incorrect assertions and generated text documentation.
- Romano et al. and the DynaCity experiment were not read beyond abstract or summary; effect magnitudes and participant mix are unverified.
- No study found on whether marking uncertainty or provenance on a software diagram changes how much developers trust it.
- No longitudinal evidence that viewing diagrams builds a durable model in engineers (unchanged from round 1).

### What remains unknown (state plainly)
- Whether predicting change impact, with feedback, improves engineers' understanding or judgment at all. The direct studies measured prediction accuracy; none trained it under controlled conditions.
- Whether any gain accumulates over months, and how fast it decays when the practice stops. The Cochrane review of professional feedback explicitly leaves sustainability outside its scope.
- Whether gains carry to a different system. The one software finding and the general transfer literature both point to no.
- Whether calibration learned on change predictions improves an engineer's decisions (for example what to review closely), as opposed to only lowering stated confidence.
- Which scoring rule, if any, helps learning or retention in humans.
- What keeps professionals using an optional effortful tool for months. No direct study found in any profession.
- Whether a per-change diagram helps comprehension, and how much a wrong one harms it. No controlled study of C4, change diagrams, or generated architecture diagrams was found.
- Whether explaining a prediction to a colleague adds to predicting alone, in professionals.
