# Human factors of supervising automation: what erodes understanding, and what keeps it

Researcher notes, 16 November 2026. Scope: aviation, process control, driving, medicine and human-AI decision studies, read for what they say about a developer who supervises a coding agent, predicts the effect of a change, and then sees a picture of what happened.

How to read each finding. The square bracket after a finding gives: source type; who was studied and on what task; my strength rating (strong, moderate, weak); and how I read the number.

- **full text** means I opened the paper (or its accepted manuscript) in this session and read the figure there.
- **abstract only** means I read the publisher or PubMed abstract and nothing else.
- **secondary** means the figure comes from another paper's description or a search summary, and I did not see it in the source.

Two terms used throughout. *Situation awareness* is the operator's grasp of what is going on, in three levels: perceiving the elements, comprehending what they mean, and projecting what happens next ([Endsley 1995, abstract only](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779049543); the three levels are named in [the 2026 probe study abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/15553434261423626)). *Automation bias* is following automated advice in place of looking for and weighing the evidence oneself ([Mosier et al. 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11540946/)).

## 1. The out-of-the-loop problem and loss of situation awareness: what is measured, how large, in whom

### Takeaway
More automation gives better routine performance and worse performance when the automation fails, and the penalty becomes much steeper once the automation chooses the action instead of only analysing information. A coding agent chooses and carries out the action, so the developer sits on the costly side of that boundary. The evidence is consistent in direction, but it is mostly student samples in short laboratory tasks, with rank correlations and no pooled effect size in standard units.

### Cited Findings
- **The "lumberjack" meta-analysis.** Across 18 experiments, a higher degree of automation went with better routine performance (Kendall's tau = +.51, p < .001, 16 studies) and worse performance when the operator had to take over after a failure (tau = −.34, p = .03, 9 studies). Situation awareness fell with more automation (tau = −.29, p = .04, 11 studies), but unevenly: 5 studies showed a loss, 4 showed none, 2 showed a gain. Subjective workload fell weakly (tau = −.24, p = .05, 12 studies). The authors used rank correlations because the studies' scales could not be compared, so there is no pooled d. [Meta-analysis; mixed domains, all but four studies used students; moderate; full text] — [Onnasch, Wickens, Li and Manzey 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720813501549), read at [TU Berlin repository copy](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/bitstreams/6a0b7962-cb5f-4317-9d07-ee11707dd51f/download)
- **The boundary that matters.** In the six studies that moved automation across the line from "analyses information" to "selects the action", the routine benefit rose to tau = +.68 and the failure cost to tau = −.90. The authors' explanation is the generation effect: "Actively choosing actions manually ... supports SA in a way that supports the manual performance in case of automation breakdown. When that choice is removed, the automation failure response suffers." They also conclude there is "no clear optimum of automation support"; the routine gain and the failure cost trade off directly. [Same source; post hoc analysis of 6 studies; weak to moderate; full text] — [Onnasch et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720813501549)
- **Original out-of-the-loop study.** Automating a navigation task with an expert system produced lower situation awareness and slower decisions after the expert system failed. Keeping the operator in partial control reduced the loss. The authors attribute the loss mainly to "the shift from active to passive processing". [Controlled study; navigation task; moderate; abstract only, no figures read] — [Endsley and Kiris 1995](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779064555)
- **Levels of automation.** With 30 participants on a dynamic control task, automating the *implementation* step helped most under normal conditions but "removal of the operator from task implementation is detrimental to performance recovery if the automated system fails". Sharing option generation between human and computer made performance worse than either alone. [Controlled study; 30 participants, laboratory control task; moderate; abstract only] — [Endsley and Kaber 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10048306/)
- **Complacency is large and tied to consistency.** When automation reliability was constant, operators detected 33% of automation failures; when reliability varied, they detected 82%. The same failures were detected about 97% of the time when monitoring was the only task. The effect appeared only under multitasking. [Review reporting Parasuraman, Molloy and Singh 1993; students and pilots on a multi-task flight simulation; strong for the phenomenon; full text of the review, secondary for the original] — [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055), read at [TU Berlin repository copy](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/bitstreams/cafd2873-814b-4c59-bab1-addd42e249d2/download)
- **Experts are not immune.** Complacency was found in pilots with about 440 flight hours, in general aviation pilots using a real cockpit alerting system, and in experienced air traffic controllers. Up to 60 minutes of extra practice did not reduce it. The review's summary: complacency "is found in both naive and expert participants and cannot be overcome with simple practice". [Review; strong; full text] — [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055)
- **Controllers with a conflict aid.** Experienced controllers did better and reported less workload with a reliable conflict-detection aid, but "detection of a particular conflict was better under manual conditions than under automated conditions when the automation was imperfect". [Controlled study; experienced air traffic controllers; moderate; abstract only] — [Metzger and Parasuraman 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15960085/)
- **Wrong automation is worse than absent automation.** After several trials of perfect aiding in a process control simulation, an aid that gave wrong advice damaged diagnostic accuracy much more than an aid that went silent. [Controlled study; laboratory process control; moderate; abstract only] — [Wickens, Clegg, Vieane and Sebok 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25886768/)
- **Bainbridge's two cognitive losses.** First, long-term knowledge: "efficient retrieval of knowledge from long-term memory depends on frequency of use", and "this type of knowledge develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness". Second, working knowledge of the current state: the operator holds "not raw data about the process state, but results of making predictions and decisions about the process which will be useful in future situations", and this "takes time to build up", so an operator who must act quickly "can only do so on the basis of minimum information". She also notes that current systems "are riding on" the skills of former manual operators, "which later generations of operators cannot be expected to have". [Opinion and review; process control and flight decks; conceptual, not a measurement; full text] — [Bainbridge 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8), read at [archived copy](https://ckrybus.com/static/papers/Bainbridge_1983_Automatica.pdf)
- **The same argument thirty years on.** Strauch argues the ironies remain unresolved and still show up in accidents. Endsley states an "automation conundrum": as autonomy becomes more reliable and robust, operators' situation awareness falls and they are less likely to be able to take over. [Reviews; opinion-level synthesis; abstract only for both] — [Strauch 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/THMS.2017.2732506); [Endsley 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28146676/)
- **Mind wandering as a mechanism.** In a Boeing 747-400 simulator, 16 airline pilots reported task-unrelated thought 20.0% of the time under autoflight, 11.7% under manual control, and 6.9% when flying manually from raw data. [Controlled study; 16 airline pilots, mean 17,844 flight hours; moderate, small sample; full text] — [Casner, Geven, Recker and Schooler 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814535628), read at [archived copy](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2014-casner.pdf). A review links mind wandering to the out-of-the-loop problem more generally. [Review; full text skimmed] — [Gouraud, Delorme and Berberian 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5633607/)
- **A dissent on generality.** Three full-scope nuclear simulator studies with licensed crews found the expected benefit of making automation visible for component-level automation, but the third study "failed to replicate these findings for automation that assumed control for executing procedural actions". The same authors argue that the laboratory idea of "automation failure" is too narrow to predict real events. [Controlled studies with licensed nuclear crews; moderate; abstract only] — [Skraaning and Jamieson 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31834815/); [Skraaning and Jamieson 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/15553434231189375)

### Inferences
- What carries to software: the direction of the trade-off, the mechanism (passive processing replaces active generation), and Bainbridge's point that understanding is built by making predictions and decisions, not by watching. A developer who only approves agent changes is in the passive condition of these studies.
- What carries only in part: the size. The laboratory failures are single, sudden and time-critical. A developer usually has minutes or hours, can read the diff, and can revert. The "takeover within seconds" penalty does not apply. The slow loss of system knowledge does.
- What may not carry: complacency was strongest when automation was almost never wrong and the operator was busy with other tasks. Coding agents are wrong often enough that developers see failures regularly, which the variable-reliability result suggests should protect monitoring. Against that, developers who run several agents at once recreate the multitasking condition under which complacency appears.
- The product asks the developer to choose (predict) before the agent acts. That restores a generation step, which is the element Onnasch et al. identify as protective. This is an inference from their explanation, not a tested result.

### Gaps
- I could not read Endsley and Kiris 1995 or Kaber and Endsley 2004 in full (publisher blocked, author copy unreachable), so I give no figures from them.
- No pooled standardised effect size exists for automation's effect on situation awareness; Onnasch et al. say their SA conclusion "needs to be treated with caution" for lack of power.
- I found no long-term field measurement of situation awareness in operators over months; the evidence is from sessions of minutes to hours.

## 2. Measurement by probe: SAGAT, SPAM, and whether a probe is also an intervention

### Takeaway
Freeze-and-ask probes are well-established as a *measure*, and the claim that they do not disturb performance is contested on method. The direct evidence that probes *improve* awareness is thin: one 2026 study found probes changed behaviour in the moment but did not transfer, with one exception, the projection (prediction) probes. Stronger evidence that prediction practice builds anticipation comes from driver hazard-perception training, where prediction exercises work but are the weakest component when used alone.

### Cited Findings
- **What the probes are.** SAGAT momentarily interrupts a simulation of the task and queries the operator on their situation awareness, covering perception, comprehension and projection. SPAM and other real-time probes ask during the task without stopping it ([Salmon et al. review, full text](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1422/1/Situation_awareness_measurement_Salmon_et_al.pdf), which calls freeze probes "highly intrusive"). Endsley's two original studies concluded the freeze technique "is not intrusive on normal subject behavior during the trial and does not suffer from limitations of human memory". [Controlled studies; abstract only] — [Endsley 1995, measurement paper](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779049499)
- **Endsley's meta-analysis of 243 studies.** SAGAT was sensitive to manipulations in 94% of studies, SPAM in 64%, other real-time probes in 73%. SAGAT and SPAM predicted performance equally. SPAM was intrusive on performance in 40% of the studies examined. [Meta-analysis by the method's author; 243 studies, many domains; moderate; abstract only] — [Endsley 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720819875376)
- **That meta-analysis is disputed.** A re-analysis found that Endsley counted a paper as predictive if at least one effect was significant, and averaged only significant effects. On a 38-paper set, the significance-filtered mean correlation between situation awareness and performance was r = 0.46; using all 241 reported effects it was r = 0.29, with a median sample of 24. The critics add that the intrusiveness conclusion rests on "vote-counting using p-values, with non-significance also incorrectly equated with no effect". [Methodological critique; full text] — [Bakdash, Marusich, Kenworthy, Twedt and Zaroukian 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7783317/)
- **Awareness predicts performance only weakly.** A separate meta-analysis of 678 effects from 77 papers found a mean correlation of r = 0.26 between situation awareness and performance, with a plausible range for individual effects of r = −0.15 to 0.60. The authors warn that "interventions that improve SA may not correspond to meaningful improvements in task performance". [Meta-analysis; moderate to strong; abstract only] — [Bakdash et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/1463922X.2021.1921310)
- **Probes as an intervention, tested directly.** Novices on an en-route air traffic control task were given SPAM probes at one of the three awareness levels, all levels, or none. The probes changed behaviour in the scenario where they were asked, but "these effects did not systematically transfer to a subsequent scenario". "Only the projection group continued to use the same strategy developed in the first scenario." The authors' conclusion is that real-time probes "elicit short-term reactivity but fail to produce lasting impacts, limiting their utility for SA training". [Controlled study; inexperienced participants, simulated air traffic control; weak to moderate, one study; abstract only] — [Reactivity Effects of Situational Awareness SPAM Questionnaires on Enroute ATC, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/15553434261423626)
- **Freeze plus reflection as training.** Forty police cadets were split into a group trained with scenario freezes and reflection on the awareness levels, and a group given ordinary skill training; the freeze group showed better awareness. [Controlled study; 40 police cadets, shooting simulator; weak; secondary, from a search summary of the abstract] — [Saus et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327876mp1803s_2)
- **Prediction training outside automation: driver hazard perception.** A meta-analysis of 57 papers found training improved hazard perception with g = 0.78 for drivers, 0.97 for cyclists, 0.64 for pedestrians and 0.42 for motorcyclists. "Active training and single sessions were found to yield more consistent significant improvements." [Meta-analysis; road users; strong for the skill measure; abstract only] — [Prabhakharan, Bennett, Hurden and Crundall 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38701558/)
- **"What happens next" exercises are the weakest single component.** With 233 novice drivers, all four training types beat placebo immediately. The full package (prediction exercises plus expert and self-generated commentary) gave the largest gain and prediction exercises alone the smallest; prediction-only was significantly worse than expert commentary both immediately and a week later. All effects decayed over the week; only the full package stayed significant. [Controlled study; 233 novice drivers, video tests; moderate; abstract only] — [Wetton, Hill and Horswill 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23474238/)
- **Experts improve, and do not notice.** Highly experienced drivers improved on a hazard perception test after 20 minutes of video training, and the gain held after at least a week. Their self-ratings did not track their measured skill, and trained drivers "did not rate their performance ... as having improved". [Controlled study; experienced mid-age drivers; moderate; abstract only] — [Horswill, Taylor, Newnam, Wetton and Hill 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23314117/)

### Inferences
- The product's structure (stop, ask for a projection, then show the outcome) is a SAGAT freeze at level 3 with feedback added. SAGAT itself gives no feedback, so the measurement literature says little about learning. The one study that used probes as training found the projection probes were the only ones whose effect persisted, which is a small point in the product's favour.
- The hazard-perception result is the best-powered evidence on prediction-with-feedback for anticipating a dynamic system, and it cautions that prediction alone is weaker than prediction combined with an expert account of what to look for. The product's drawn picture of what happened plays the role of the expert commentary. That pairing, not the prediction prompt alone, is what the driving evidence supports.
- The weak link from awareness to performance (r about 0.26) means an improvement in prediction accuracy inside the tool should not be assumed to produce better engineering outcomes. It would need its own measurement.
- Horswill's finding that experts improve without feeling it suggests the tool should show the developer their own record, since self-report will under-detect gains.

### Gaps
- I found no study in aviation, process control or driving automation in which operators were asked to predict the automation's next action as a standing intervention and were then compared with a control group on later understanding. This is the central missing test.
- I could not read Endsley 2021 in full, so I cannot say how many studies its "not intrusive" verdict for SAGAT rests on.
- The Saus et al. result is secondary; I did not see its statistics.

## 3. Skill decay under automation: what decays fastest and what prevents it

### Takeaway
Cognitive skills decay faster than physical ones, and using an automated aid does not count as practice. In airline pilots, hand-flying was largely intact while the thinking that goes with manual flight (tracking position, deciding the next step, recognising failures) had degraded. A developer's skills are almost entirely of the fast-decaying kind.

### Cited Findings
- **Skill-decay meta-analysis.** Across 189 data points from 53 articles, skill loss grew from near zero immediately after training (the abstract gives d = −0.01) to d = −1.4 after more than a year without use. Cognitive tasks decayed more than physical tasks (−1.15 against −0.75). Accuracy-based tasks decayed more than three times as much as speed-based ones (−1.00 against −0.32). Recall tests showed slightly more loss than recognition tests (−0.96 against −0.85). Overlearning was too rarely reported to test well. [Meta-analysis; mostly training studies, many with students or military trainees; strong for direction, old; full text, from a scanned copy with imperfect text recognition] — [Arthur, Bennett, Stanush and McNelly 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327043hup1101_3), read at [archived scan](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1998-arthur.pdf)
- **Newer, larger meta-analysis of procedural skills.** From 1,344 effect sizes in 457 reports, the loss grew by 0.08 SD per month for accuracy measures and 0.06 SD per month for speed and mixed measures. "Half of initial skill acquisition performance gains were lost after approximately 6.5 months for accuracy, 13 months for speed." Intermittent chances to perform were one of the moderators. [Meta-analysis; procedural skills with a motor component; strong; abstract only] — [Tatel and Ackerman 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40455501/)
- **Pilots: hands intact, thinking degraded.** Sixteen airline pilots flew a 747-400 simulator. Instrument scanning and manual control were "mostly intact, even when pilots reported that they were infrequently practiced". When they had to navigate without the map display, 7 of 16 missed the missed-approach point, 6 of 16 flew the wrong missed-approach heading, and only 1 completed the arrival, approach and missed approach without a significant error. Errors correlated with task-unrelated thought, r(14) = .56. The authors conclude that retention of the cognitive skills "may depend on the degree to which pilots remain actively engaged in supervising the automation". [Controlled study; 16 airline pilots; moderate, small sample, no pre-automation baseline; full text] — [Casner et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814535628)
- **Recent practice beats total experience.** Among 126 airline pilots flying a manual raw-data approach, fleet type was the strongest predictor of deviation: long-haul pilots, who fly few sectors a month with heavy automation use, did worse. "Recent flight practice is a significantly stronger predictor for fine-motor flying performance than the time period since flight school or even the total or type-specific flight experience." [Cross-sectional study; 126 randomly selected airline pilots; moderate; full text of accepted manuscript, abstract-level figures] — [Haslbeck and Hoermann 2016](https://elib.dlr.de/104258/). An earlier study found the same link between very recent flying and manual control strategy. [Cross-sectional study; airline pilots; abstract only] — [Ebbatson, Harris, Huddlestone and Sears 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20099179/)
- **Using the automation is not practice.** Students did a flight-planning calculation five times over nine weeks, either always by hand, alternating, or with an automated aid in between. All groups declined on the manual task; the automation group's drop (−0.40) was twice the manual group's (−0.20), p = .045. The journal version reports the automation group also had the highest workload at the end. [Controlled study; 59 undergraduates enrolled, 46 finished, a simple calculation task; weak to moderate; full text of thesis, abstract of journal paper] — [Volz 2018 thesis](https://www.imse.iastate.edu/files/2018/07/VolzKatie-thesis.pdf); [Volz and Dorneich 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1555343420962897)
- **Refreshers preserve what they exercise.** In simulated process control, hands-on practice and skill demonstration preserved the skill. Symbolic rehearsal and a knowledge test preserved the knowledge "but showed moderate skill decay". [Two controlled studies, N = 68 each; laboratory process control start-up; moderate; abstract only] — [Kluge and Frank 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24382262/)
- **Medicine, with AI.** In four Polish centres, the adenoma detection rate in colonoscopies done *without* AI fell from 28.4% (226/795) before the centres introduced AI to 22.4% (145/648) after, an absolute change of −6.0 points (95% CI −10.5 to −1.6, p = 0.009; adjusted odds ratio 0.69, 0.53 to 0.89). All 19 endoscopists had done more than 2,000 colonoscopies. [Observational before-and-after study; 19 experienced endoscopists, 1,443 patients; moderate, not randomised for this comparison, the authors note patient mix and workload may differ; full text of accepted manuscript] — [Budzyń et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-1253(25)00133-5), read at [Warwick repository copy](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/191005)
- **A theory paper, not data.** The widely cited paper arguing that AI assistance may speed skill decay in experts and hinder skill growth in learners, without either group noticing, is a theoretical perspective with no new measurements. [Opinion; full text] — [Macnamara et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11239631/)

### Inferences
- What carries: the ranking. The skills that decay fastest are cognitive, accuracy-based and recall-dependent. Predicting what a change will do to a system is all three. Typing speed and editor fluency are the analogue of hand-flying and are the least at risk.
- What carries: practice must exercise the skill itself. Watching an agent work, or reading its summary, is the analogue of using the flight-planning aid. A prediction is a recall-type act, which the retention literature treats as the harder and more protective form.
- Practice schedule: the half-life figures (about 6.5 months for accuracy) come from skills left wholly unused. They indicate decay is a matter of months, not days, so occasional prediction (not on every change) could be enough to hold a skill. This is an extrapolation; none of these studies tested a schedule in software work.
- What does not carry: the pilot studies measure a skill that was once fully trained to a licensing standard. A junior developer who has never built the system model has nothing to retain. Bainbridge's "later generations" warning applies to them, and retention studies say nothing about it.

### Gaps
- No study I found measures decay of a software engineer's system understanding under agent use over months with a control group. The other research notes in this folder cover the nearest software evidence.
- The Volz thesis baseline for the automation group may reflect aided performance on the first trial; the journal abstract says the first trial was manual. I could not reconcile the two.

## 4. Interventions that work: effect sizes and limits

### Takeaway
The best-supported intervention is a short return to doing the task oneself: ten minutes of manual control raised later detection of automation failures. Display-based approaches (transparency, ecological displays) help accurate use but do not stop over-reliance on highly reliable automation, and they help people with more attentional capacity more. Training that exposes operators to automation failures helps, but only for the kind of failure they saw.

### Cited Findings
- **Intermittent manual control.** Participants monitored an automated engine task for three 30-minute sessions. All groups detected few automation failures during the first 40 minutes. Groups who were handed the task back for 10 minutes mid-way "detected significantly more automation failures during the subsequent blocks under automation control". It worked whether the hand-back was scheduled or triggered by poor monitoring. [Controlled study; laboratory multi-task flight simulation; moderate; abstract only] — [Parasuraman, Mouloua and Molloy 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11536753/)
- **Doing transfers to watching; watching does not transfer to doing.** Eighteen participants either controlled or monitored a dynamic system. "The skill acquired by controlling transferred positively to the monitoring mode, producing enhanced detection performance. There was no transfer from the monitoring mode to the controlling mode." [Controlled study; 18 participants, tracking task; moderate, old and small; abstract only] — [Kessel and Wickens 1982](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872088202400106)
- **Adaptive automation with professionals.** Fourteen professional air traffic controllers worked a highly automated interface with and without a system that lowered automation when brain and eye measures showed vigilance dropping. The adaptive system "was able to counteract this negative effect by keeping the ATCo more involved". [Controlled study; 14 professional controllers; weak to moderate; abstract only] — [Di Flumeri et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6743225/)
- **Transparency: the positive case.** In a multi-vehicle management task where the agent was wrong 3 times in 8, performance, trust and usability rose with the level of transparency (what the agent is doing, why, and what it expects to happen), with no rise in workload or response time. [Controlled study; within-subject, laboratory participants; moderate; abstract only] — [Mercado et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26867556/)
- **Transparency: the systematic review.** Seventeen experiments show "a promising effect of automation transparency on Situation Awareness and operator performance, without the cost of added mental workload", for people who respond to agent proposals or supervise agents. Effects varied by task, agent type and how well the information was integrated into the main display. [Systematic review of 17 experiments; moderate, no pooled effect size; full text] — [van de Merwe, Mallam and Nazir 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10756021/)
- **Transparency: three limits.** (a) Under time pressure (12 seconds against 25), accuracy fell and "high transparency did not mitigate the negative impacts of high time pressure". (b) Transparency removed the under-use of low-reliability automation but "did not mitigate the misuse associated with high-reliability automation". (c) Transparency "increased the accuracy of automation use for participants with higher [attention control capacity], but did not benefit participants with lower". [Three controlled studies; laboratory vehicle-management task; moderate; abstract only] — [Tatasciore and Loft 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37757606/); [Gegoff et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37635389/); [Gegoff et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40795581/)
- **Transparency does not generalise to agents that act.** See the nuclear result in section 1: the benefit held for component-level automation and failed to replicate for automation that executed procedures. [Controlled studies with licensed crews; abstract only] — [Skraaning and Jamieson 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31834815/)
- **Ecological interface design.** A literature review concluded that in problem-solving situations this approach (displays that show the system's functional structure and constraints) improves performance over industry practice, but that no full-fidelity test with professionals had been run. [Review; abstract only] — [Vicente 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12118874/). The later full-scope test with licensed nuclear operators found better situation awareness only "in scenarios that did not have procedural support, primarily in the detection phases"; in other conditions there was no gain and occasional under-performance. [Controlled study; licensed nuclear operators, full-scope simulator; moderate; abstract only] — [Burns et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18767525/)
- **Failure-exposure training.** Twenty-four participants ran a process control simulation with a diagnostic aid. Those who experienced the aid missing faults during training checked the alarm function more and made fewer omission errors than those merely told it could fail. The training did not change checking of the aid's diagnoses or commission errors: 74% of participants followed a wrong diagnosis. [Controlled study; 24 participants; moderate; full text] — [Bahner, Elepfandt and Manzey 2008](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/handle/11303/8827). A companion study found that experiencing wrong diagnoses in training did reduce complacency toward diagnoses, though checking stayed below what was needed and 21% still made a commission error. [Controlled study; full text of the review, secondary for the original] — [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055)
- **Failure exposure can backfire.** Forty-five participants trained for 3.5 hours with reliable, misdiagnosing or fault-missing automation and were tested a week later. "Automation bias was very high when operators trained on miss-prone automation encountered a failure of the diagnostic system", and the trust differences produced by training "disappeared during the testing session". [Controlled study; 45 participants, process control; moderate; abstract only] — [Sauer, Chavaillaz and Wastell 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26374396/)
- **Variable and unpredictable simulator training.** Ten pilots who practised failures in an unpredictable, varied way took longer in practice but showed "superior understanding and performance" in a later surprise scenario than ten who practised the same failures predictably. [Controlled study; 20 pilots; moderate, small; abstract only] — [Landman et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29913086/)
- **Routine recurrent training is brittle.** Eighteen airline pilots handled abnormal events well when presented as in training, but "when the abnormal events were presented unexpectedly, pilots' responses were less appropriate and showed great variability". The authors conclude that such training yields "rote-memorized skills that are specific to the training situation". [Controlled study; 18 airline pilots; moderate; abstract only] — [Casner, Geven and Williams 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23829023/)
- **Attention training.** Variable-priority training gave better multitasking and "a trend for a reduction in the automation complacency effect". [Review reporting one study; weak; full text of the review] — [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055)

### Inferences
- A prediction before the agent acts is the cheapest available form of "intermittent manual control" for a developer: the cognitive part of the task is done by hand, without writing the code. The Parasuraman and Kessel results are the closest support for the product's premise in this literature. The match is imperfect: in those studies the operator did the whole task, not a forecast of it.
- The drawn picture of what happened is a transparency display. The evidence says such displays help accurate use but do not fix over-reliance on a usually-right system, lose their value under time pressure, and help high-capacity users most. So the picture alone should not be expected to keep understanding intact. This supports pairing it with the prediction step.
- Failure-exposure results imply the tool should make prediction misses visible and specific. They also warn that what is learned is narrow: people become wary of the kind of error they have seen and stay complacent about other kinds.
- Landman and Casner 2013 imply the questions must vary. A fixed prediction form asked the same way each time would train the form, not the skill.
- What does not carry: adaptive automation driven by brain or eye measures, and designs built around a seconds-long takeover.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis of adaptive automation or of intermittent manual control with a pooled effect size.
- No study tested how long the benefit of a manual interval lasts beyond the same session.
- I did not read the original Chen et al. transparency model paper; my account of it comes from the reviews above.

## 5. Mental models of automated systems, and whether asking for predictions improves them

### Takeaway
Experienced operators hold incomplete models of their automation and monitor outcomes far more than they monitor what the automation is set up to do. Training built on the system's logic improves stated understanding. Within classic human factors I found no controlled test of "predict the automation's behaviour, then see it" as the training method. The nearest tests are in human-AI research, and they show that committing to a judgement first changes reliance but does not by itself produce learning.

### Cited Findings
- **Mode confusion.** Flexible, highly autonomous systems require the supervisor to "track which mode the automation is in and what it is doing", and when modes multiply without support, new error forms appear. This is drawn from a series of studies of pilots in glass cockpits. [Review of the authors' empirical studies; airline pilots; moderate; abstract only] — [Sarter and Woods 1995](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779049516)
- **How pilots monitor.** Twenty experienced 747-400 pilots, tracked by eye movements for an hour, monitored basic flight parameters much more than automation status. They "frequently fail to verify manual mode selections or notice automatic mode changes" and at other times "do not process mode annunciations in sufficient depth to understand their implications". Low observability and gaps in understanding both contributed. [Controlled observational study; 20 experienced airline pilots, full-mission simulator; moderate; abstract only] — [Sarter, Mumaw and Wickens 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17552302/)
- **Training the model directly.** Drivers given a short training programme on a partial driving-automation system "both self-report and exhibit an improved understanding of the automation system" and reported a higher inclination to retake control in critical situations. [Controlled study; simulator, between groups; weak to moderate, proof of concept; abstract only] — [Krampell, Solís-Marcos and Hjälmdahl 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31731093/)
- **Prior experience shortens takeover.** Across 129 studies of taking over from automated driving, a shorter takeover time went with having experienced another takeover earlier in the experiment, among other factors. [Meta-analysis; 129 driving studies; strong for the association; abstract only] — [Zhang, de Winter, Varotto, Happee and Martens 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trf.2019.04.020)
- **Committing first, in AI-assisted decisions.** Nineteen veterinary radiologists read X-rays with an AI tool. Those who had to register a provisional answer before seeing the AI's were "less likely to agree with the AI regardless of whether the advice is accurate", and rated the AI advice as less useful. Requiring the provisional answer "did not lengthen the time participants spent on the task". [Controlled study; 19 veterinary radiologists; weak to moderate, small; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Fogliato et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09696)
- **Committing first did not produce learning.** In three experiments on nutrition decisions with a simulated AI, people who saw a recommendation and explanation decided better but did not learn. People who "first made their own choice, and only then saw a recommendation and an explanation" (268 participants) also decided better but showed "no learning". Only people given the explanation *without* the recommendation, who had to reach the decision themselves, showed both better decisions and learning gains (221 participants, with a replication of 270). [Three controlled experiments; online volunteers and crowd workers, nutrition judgements; moderate; full text] — [Gajos and Mamykina 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05402)
- **Tutorials built from the model.** Randomised experiments found that tutorials teaching people the patterns a model uses improved their performance on a text-classification task, with and without live assistance, and that tutorials from simple models helped more than those from a more accurate deep model. [Controlled experiments; crowd workers; moderate; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Lai, Liu and Tan 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05871)

### Inferences
- The eye-tracking result maps onto code review: developers check the outcome (tests pass, the feature works) more than the configuration of what was changed and why. A system-level picture of the change addresses the "low observability" half of that finding. It does not address the "gaps in understanding" half.
- Gajos and Mamykina's second experiment is the closest test of the product's core move, and its result is a caution. Stating a choice and then being shown the answer improved the decision but did not teach. Learning came when people had to work from the evidence to a conclusion with no answer handed over. For the product, this argues that after a wrong prediction the developer should be made to locate the cause in the picture, not be shown a "correct answer" label.
- That experiment used strangers on a one-off nutrition task. A developer predicting effects on their own system has prior knowledge and a reason to care, which are conditions the learning-science notes in this folder associate with stronger prediction effects. So the null may not carry. It still sets the default expectation.
- Fogliato's result implies a cost the product should expect: people who commit first rate the automation as less useful.

### Gaps
- No controlled study in aviation, process control or driving tests prediction of automation behaviour as the training method. This is the same gap as in section 2.
- I could not obtain Sarter and Woods's pilot surveys on automation surprises or the Sarter, Woods and Billings 1997 chapter, so I give no figures on how common such surprises are.
- I could not open the Bansal et al. 2019 paper on mental models of an AI's error boundary (site blocked automated access).

## 6. Automation bias in decision support and what reduces it

### Takeaway
Wrong advice from a decision aid raises the chance of a wrong decision by about a quarter in clinical studies, and both novices and experts are affected. What reduces it: feeling personally accountable for accuracy, being told about and shown system errors, less prominent advice, and having to engage before seeing the answer. What does not reduce it: instructions to verify, a second person, or more practice.

### Cited Findings
- **Size in healthcare.** Pooling four clinical studies, erroneous decision-support advice raised the risk of an incorrect decision with a risk ratio of 1.26 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.44). In four prospective studies, clinicians changed a correct answer to an incorrect one after consulting the system in 6% to 11% of cases. The review covered 74 studies. [Systematic review with a small meta-analysis; clinicians; moderate, four studies pooled; full text] — [Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/)
- **Mitigators in that review.** Accountability manipulations reduced bias in two studies, and in a third only self-perceived accountability did. Showing the system's reasoning increased appropriate reliance in one study. Less prominent advice, less on-screen detail, updated confidence levels, and "supportive information rather than commands" reduced bias. Complacency "is more clearly reduced by training than" automation bias. [Same source; full text] — [Goddard et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/)
- **It depends on how hard checking is.** Of 40 studies, 81% of those testing for omission errors and 91% of those testing for commission errors found bias. Bias appeared in single tasks too, "typically involving diagnosis rather than monitoring, and with high verification complexity". Median sample size was 30, and only 9 studies tested bias against a control with statistics. [Systematic review; 40 studies, mostly students; moderate; full text] — [Lyell and Coiera 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7651899/)
- **Accountability.** In 181 non-pilots, those told they would have to justify their overall performance or accuracy made fewer omission and commission errors and checked the automation more. Those made accountable for speed checked less. A second crew member did not reduce bias, and "neither of the training and instruction interventions had any effect". [Review reporting Skitka, Mosier and Burdick 2000 and related studies; full text of the review, secondary for the originals] — [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055). In glass-cockpit pilots, imposed accountability had no significant effect, but pilots who reported an internalised sense of accountability double-checked more and erred less. [Controlled study; airline pilots; moderate; abstract only] — [Mosier, Skitka, Heers and Burdick 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11540946/)
- **Three routes to a commission error.** Incomplete cross-checking, active discounting of contradictory information, and looking at contradictory information without taking it in. Automation failures shaped later behaviour more strongly than runs of correct performance. [Two controlled experiments; laboratory process control; moderate; abstract only] — [Manzey, Reichenbach and Onnasch 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1555343411433844)
- **Experts with modern AI.** Twenty-seven radiologists read mammograms with a purported AI. When the AI's suggested category was wrong, correct ratings fell from 79.7% to 19.8% for inexperienced readers, 81.3% to 24.8% for moderately experienced, and 82.3% to 45.5% for very experienced readers. [Controlled experiment; 27 radiologists, 50 mammograms; moderate; abstract only] — [Dratsch et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37129490/)
- **Telling people about errors, against telling them they are responsible.** In AI-supported personnel selection, people informed about system errors verified more and rated their decisions higher; people informed of their responsibility "unexpectedly, did not". More verification went with better decisions. [Controlled experiment, 3 × 2 design; moderate; abstract only] — [Kupfer et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37089740/)
- **Human plus AI overall.** Across 370 effect sizes from 106 experiments, human-AI combinations did worse than the better of the human or the AI alone (Hedges' g = −0.23, 95% CI −0.39 to −0.07) and better than humans alone (g = 0.64, 0.53 to 0.74). Losses were concentrated in decision tasks and in cases where the AI alone outperformed the human. [Meta-analysis; many tasks, 2020 to 2023; strong; full text] — [Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/)
- **Explanations reduce over-reliance only when checking is cheap relative to doing.** Five studies (N = 731) on a maze task showed over-reliance moved with task difficulty, explanation difficulty and payment, which the authors read as people choosing whether engaging is worth the effort. [Controlled experiments; crowd workers; moderate; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Vasconcelos et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823)

### Inferences
- What carries: agent output is the "command" form of advice (a finished change) that the reviews associate with more bias than supportive information. Verifying a multi-file change is a high-verification-complexity task, which is where Lyell and Coiera found bias concentrated. A system-level picture that lowers the cost of checking is consistent with the Vasconcelos account.
- The accountability evidence favours a prediction that is recorded and compared with the outcome, since that makes accuracy the thing the developer answers for. Mosier's and Kupfer's results caution that accountability imposed from outside is weaker than accountability the person adopts.
- What does not carry directly: the clinical figures come from one-shot judgements with a single right answer. A code change has many properties and partial correctness, so a rate such as "26% more errors" should not be quoted for software.

### Gaps
- I did not read the Skitka et al. papers in the original; their abstracts were not available through the indexes I could reach.

## 7. Applications to software engineering with AI or agents, 2023 to 2026

### Takeaway
The human factors framing has reached software engineering mainly as position papers and qualitative studies. The 2026 empirical work shows developers skim agent traces and that reviewers approve agent pull requests more readily as they gain experience, though one controlled comparison attributes thinner reviews to an ecosystem-wide trend. One 2026 position paper recommends the product's exact mechanism (record your own view before seeing the agent's) but tests nothing. I found no controlled experiment that measures a developer's situation awareness, or tests a prediction step, while supervising a coding agent.

### Cited Findings
- **Cognitive forcing functions: effect and cost.** With 199 crowd workers on a nutrition task, three designs that force engagement (ask for the AI on demand, decide first then update, or wait 30 seconds) were compared with simple explanations. When the AI was wrong, correct identification of the key ingredient was 0.49 with no AI, 0.08 with simple explanations and 0.27 with forcing functions (d = .66 between the last two); over-reliance on that sub-task fell from 0.64 to 0.48 (d = .36). Overall preference did not differ significantly (3.78 for explanations, 3.62 for forcing functions, on a 5-point scale), but the forcing designs were rated as more complex (2.95 against 2.64), and trust and preference were negatively correlated with performance on the AI's wrong cases. People high in need for cognition benefited more, and among them the forcing designs were trusted less (3.56 against 3.89, p = .02) and preferred less (3.44 against 3.75, p = .03); people low in need for cognition showed no such difference. [Controlled experiment; 199 crowd workers, 15-minute task; moderate; full text] — [Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692)
- **Note on that result.** Both AI conditions were well below the no-AI condition when the AI was wrong (0.27 and 0.08 against 0.49). The forcing functions recovered part of the loss, not all of it. [Same source; full text] — [Buçinca et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692)
- **Forcing functions on AI plans.** In a controlled experiment (n = 214) on reviewing an AI's execution plan for a writing task, a prompt to identify the plan's assumptions "most effectively reduced overreliance without increasing cognitive load"; a "what if" prompt was less effective, and combining both "may be ineffective or even detrimental". Users nonetheless "perceived the WhatIf [prompt] to be more helpful". [Controlled experiment plus 12 interviews; preprint; moderate; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Ghosh, Sarkar, Lindley and Poelitz 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18033)
- **"Ironies of generative AI".** A review maps human factors findings onto generative AI and names four causes of lost productivity: "a shift in users' roles from production to evaluation, unhelpful restructuring of workflows, interruptions, and a tendency for automation to make easy tasks easier and hard tasks harder". Programming is a main example. [Review and opinion; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Simkute, Tankelevitch et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11364). Endsley makes a parallel argument, listing five ironies of AI including difficulty understanding the AI and judging its reliability. [Opinion; abstract only] — [Endsley 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37534468/)
- **Self-reported critical thinking.** In a survey of 319 knowledge workers giving 936 examples, "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking, while higher self-confidence is associated with more critical thinking". [Survey; self-report; weak for causation; abstract only] — [Lee et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
- **Position paper recommending pre-commitment.** "Oversight degrades the overseer": the paper applies the out-of-the-loop problem to agents, quotes a developer as "cognitively distant from the code they must review", and recommends, among other measures, "pre-commitment mechanisms [that] have the user record their own view or decision before seeing the agent's recommendation", delay-and-choice mechanisms, and "domain skill maintenance exercises" done without AI. It reports no experiment. [Opinion; preprint, version dated 12 November 2026; full text] — [Mitchell, Ghosh and Passi 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23642)
- **How developers actually oversee agents.** Interviews with 17 experienced developers found four kinds of oversight work: control set up in advance, co-planning, real-time monitoring and review afterwards. When they monitored reasoning traces at all they did so "perfunctorily", "eyeball[ing]" them, and they used heuristics such as "using test results as guarantees for code correctness". Twelve of the 17 worked at the authors' own company. [Qualitative interview study; preprint; weak to moderate; full text] — [Dhanorkar, Passi and Vorvoreanu 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05391)
- **Supervisory control mapped to coding agents.** Observation of 19 experienced developers was used to recast Sheridan's five supervisory stages as seven (plan, monitor, wait, review, teach, manual fix, update assets); developers concentrate effort in planning and hand supervisory work to other agents. [Qualitative study; preprint; weak to moderate; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Park et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24234)
- **Engagement falls during a session.** In a formative study with four software engineers, "cognitive engagement consistently declines as tasks progress" and participants attended to outputs over process, limiting later recall. The authors suggest cognitive forcing mechanisms. [Formative study; 4 engineers, workshop paper; weak; full text] — [Catalan, Dizon, Monderin and Kuang 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14225)
- **Reviewers approve more over time.** Among 400 repeat reviewers and 11,429 reviews of agent pull requests over seven months, approval rate rose from 30.1% in early reviews to 36.8% in late ones, with a 14.5-point gap between the first and tenth experience deciles and 22% fewer inline comments. Approval of human pull requests fell over the same period. [Observational repository study; preprint; moderate, not causal; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Yu et al. 2026, "Habituation at the Gate"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22721)
- **A contrary reading.** A study of 669 developers in 103 repositories, with 228 control developers, found reviews did become "faster, less detailed, and more permissive" after agents arrived, but the same change occurred in repositories without agents. The authors find "no evidence that developers reduce productive effort when working with AI agents"; pull request descriptions grew 76.8% longer and testing discipline rose slightly. [Observational study with difference-in-differences; peer-reviewed conference paper; moderate; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Hamza, Siemon and Awan 2026](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.67)
- **Related proposals.** One paper argues that diff-based review misses drift between agent actions and architectural intent and proposes an intent-level causal graph for oversight. Another proposes "situation aware" as the top level of agent proactivity. Neither reports a user study. [Opinion and design proposals; preprints; weak; full text, abstract-level findings] — [Casserini, Facchini and Ferrario 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16323); [Bui and Evangelopoulos 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06717)

### Inferences
- The two repository studies disagree on whether thinner review is caused by agents. They agree that review of agent work is getting thinner. Either way, the passive-monitoring condition the human factors literature warns about is present in practice.
- The acceptance cost is real but smaller than often stated. In Buçinca et al. overall preference did not drop significantly; the cost showed as higher perceived complexity, and as lower trust and preference among the very people who benefited. Fogliato et al. found the automation was rated less useful. Ghosh et al. found users preferred the less effective prompt. The pattern: people's liking of a forcing step is a poor guide to whether it works, so opt-in rates and satisfaction scores will understate value and may steer design the wrong way.
- The benefit of forcing functions fell on people with high need for cognition (Buçinca) and transparency helped people with high attention control (Gegoff 2025). The product should expect to help already-engaged developers most.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment measures situation awareness, mental-model accuracy or takeover performance in developers supervising coding agents. No experiment tests a prediction step before an agent's change. I searched arXiv and the web for both and found position papers and qualitative work only.
- All 2026 items except Hamza et al. are preprints or workshop papers and may change.

## 8. Transfer: do the gains carry to new situations?

### Takeaway
Transfer is the weakest part of this evidence. Gains from doing the task carry over to monitoring it; gains from failure exposure, probes and routine drills are mostly specific to what was practised and fade within weeks. Varied, unpredictable practice is the one manipulation shown to improve handling of a new situation.

### Cited Findings
- Controlling a system transferred to detecting failures while monitoring it; monitoring did not transfer to controlling. [Controlled study; abstract only] — [Kessel and Wickens 1982](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872088202400106)
- Unpredictable and variable practice improved performance in a new surprise scenario, at the cost of slower practice. [Controlled study; 20 pilots; abstract only] — [Landman et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29913086/)
- Predictable recurrent drills produced skills "specific to the training situation and that offer modest generalizability". [Controlled study; 18 airline pilots; abstract only] — [Casner et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23829023/)
- Failure-exposure effects were specific to the function that failed in training (alarm against diagnosis), and training-induced trust differences vanished within a week of testing. [Controlled studies; full text and abstract only respectively] — [Bahner, Elepfandt and Manzey 2008](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/handle/11303/8827); [Sauer et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26374396/)
- Probe-induced strategies did not transfer to a second scenario, except in the projection-probe group. [Controlled study; abstract only] — [SPAM reactivity study 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/15553434261423626)
- Hazard-perception training effects "decayed significantly" over one week; only the full package remained significant. The meta-analysis calls for research on long-term retention. [Controlled study and meta-analysis; abstract only] — [Wetton et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23474238/); [Prabhakharan et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38701558/)
- Transfer to real outcomes, one case. A United States evaluation tracked 5,251 newly licensed young drivers for 12 months after a short computer-based risk-anticipation training. Trained males had about a 23.7% lower crash rate than the male comparison group; trained females showed a non-significant 10.7% higher rate; there was no effect on time to first crash or on violations. [Large field trial; newly licensed teen drivers; moderate, mixed result; secondary, the report's host refused automated access and I read only a search summary] — [Thomas et al. 2016, NHTSA report](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/1986)
- Using an aid in place of the task gave no carry-over to doing the task unaided. [Controlled study; full text of thesis] — [Volz 2018](https://www.imse.iastate.edu/files/2018/07/VolzKatie-thesis.pdf)
- In AI-assisted decisions, learning that carried to later unaided questions appeared only when people had to reach the decision themselves from an explanation. [Controlled experiments; full text] — [Gajos and Mamykina 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05402)

### Inferences
- For the product, the transfer question is whether predicting the effect of one change improves understanding of the next, different change. The evidence predicts narrow transfer by default: a developer will get better at the kinds of change they have predicted, in the part of the system they have predicted about. Varying the kind of question and the part of the system is the only tested route to wider transfer.
- Decay over a week in the driving studies, and the vanishing of trained trust differences, imply that a prediction habit must continue at some rate to hold its effect. The evidence does not say what rate.
- None of these studies measured transfer over more than a few weeks, and none in software. Any claim that the product builds lasting, general system understanding would go beyond this literature.

### Gaps
- No study follows operators for months after an intervention to test whether understanding of the automated system is retained.
- The crash-reduction figure is secondary and applies to one sex in one trial; it should not be quoted as an established effect.
- I found no study on whether gains transfer across different automated systems (for example from one aircraft type or one agent to another).
