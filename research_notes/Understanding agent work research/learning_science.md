# Learning science of building and keeping system understanding: prediction before outcome, and diagrams

Notes dated 16 November 2026. Scope: evidence for a tool in which a developer predicts the system-level effect of an agent's change, then sees a drawn diagram of what happened.

**How to read these notes**

- Each finding carries: source type, population, strength rating, and a verification tag.
- Verification tags:
  - `[READ]` = I read the number in the primary source (full text) in this session.
  - `[ABSTRACT]` = the number came from the paper's abstract or a publisher/repository record surfaced by search; consistent across sources but full text not read.
  - `[SECONDARY]` = number comes only from a secondary summary (blog, slides, press). Treat as unverified.
- Strength rating is for the claim *as applied to this tool* (experienced engineers, system-level judgment), not for the original finding in its own setting:
  - **Strong** = replicated or meta-analytic, and shown in working professionals on judgment or behaviour.
  - **Moderate** = meta-analytic or replicated, but mostly students or mostly recall; or one good professional study.
  - **Weak** = single study, small sample, self-report, or far transfer from another setting.
  - **Very weak** = theory, opinion, or inference only.
- "STUDENT-RECALL" marks findings that are about recall or comprehension tests in students and have not been shown for professional judgment.

## 1. Program and system comprehension: how engineers form mental models, what helps, what is forgotten

### Takeaway
Comprehension of large systems mixes top-down (hypothesis-driven) and bottom-up reading, and professionals keep most system knowledge in their heads and in conversation rather than in documents. The hardest questions are about rationale and about what a change will reach, which is exactly what a system-level prediction asks for. Familiarity with code decays with time. The classic models come from small think-aloud studies and have never been tested as a training target.

### Cited Findings
- Von Mayrhauser and Vans's integrated metamodel describes large-scale code comprehension as a combination of three processes: program model, situation model, and top-down (domain) model; studies with large-scale code show both top-down and bottom-up activity. Their industrial evidence is think-aloud protocols from maintenance programmers. They described theory-building for large-scale comprehension as "in its infancy". Type: review plus small observational studies. Population: professional maintainers (small n). Strength: Moderate as description, Very weak as a basis for training. `[ABSTRACT]` — [von Mayrhauser and Vans 1995 survey, Kent State copy](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs69995-PC/papers/von_mayrhauser-1995.pdf); [Wyrich et al., contemporary review, arXiv 2310.11301](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11301)
- A systematic review of programmers' mental models finds results "most correspond" with the integrated metamodel, but the model lacks elements such as how programmers locate code and why code changed over time. Type: systematic review. Population: mixed, mostly students in underlying studies. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Heinonen et al., arXiv 2212.07763](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07763)
- fMRI work (11 participants, FSE 2017) found lower brain activation when comprehension was aided by semantic cues (beacons) than in bottom-up comprehension. A 2018 follow-up replicated the top-down versus bottom-up efficiency difference but *failed to find a significant beacon effect*. Type: small controlled studies. Population: students. Strength: Weak. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Siegmund et al. FSE 2017](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/SPP+17.pdf); [Neural Efficiency of Top-Down Program Comprehension](https://dl.gi.de/items/b1a838c6-9e5c-47e2-9502-41953667eab9/full)
- LaToza, Venolia and DeLine (ICSE 2006; two surveys and eleven interviews at Microsoft): developers keep an internal mental model of the code, share it mainly face to face and through the code, avoid written design documents, and depend on code ownership to retain implicit knowledge; asking colleagues causes interruptions. Type: survey and interviews. Population: professional developers, one company. Strength: Moderate (descriptive). `[ABSTRACT]` — [Maintaining mental models, PDF](https://www.st.cs.uni-saarland.de/edu/empirical-se/2006/PDFs/latoza06.pdf); [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=154508)
- LaToza and Myers, hard-to-answer questions about code: 179 professional developers reported 371 questions. The most frequent hard questions concerned design rationale, understanding implementations, and debugging; rationale questions were both the most frequent and not addressed by research tools. Type: survey. Population: professionals (Microsoft). Strength: Moderate (descriptive). `[SECONDARY]` for counts (author's slides) — [PLATEAU 2010 slides](https://ecs.wgtn.ac.nz/foswiki/pub/Events/PLATEAU/2010Program/plateau10-latoza-slides.pdf); [ACM record](https://www.springerprofessional.de/doi/10.1145/1937117.1937125)
- LaToza and Myers, "Developers ask reachability questions" (ICSE 2010): in a survey, 82% of developers rated at least one of 12 reachability questions (what can this code reach, what reaches it) as at least somewhat hard, on average 4.1 of 12. Type: survey plus lab and field observation. Population: professionals. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [LaToza and Myers ICSE 2010](https://memphis-cs.github.io/comp-7085-8085-2012-fall/papers/LaToza2010ICSE.pdf)
- Bacchelli and Bird (ICSE 2013, Microsoft; observation, interviews, survey, comment classification): "code and change understanding is the key aspect of code reviewing"; reviews find fewer defects than expected and deliver more knowledge transfer and team awareness. Type: mixed-methods field study. Population: professionals. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [ICSE 2013 page](https://2013.icse-conferences.org/content/expectations-outcomes-and-challenges-modern-code-review.html); [full text copy](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2018-481/readings/codereview.pdf)
- Forgetting: Krüger et al. (ICSE 2018, 60 open-source developers, self-rated familiarity) found a forgetting curve "partly applicable" to code familiarity; number of edits and share of code owned correlate moderately to strongly with familiarity. A 2019 follow-up says Ebbinghaus's model fits only when a file was implemented in a single session. Type: survey (self-report). Population: open-source developers. Strength: Weak to Moderate (self-rated familiarity, not tested knowledge). `[ABSTRACT]` — [Krüger et al. 2018](https://jacobkrueger.github.io/assets/papers/Krueger2018Remember.pdf); [Understanding How Programmers Forget](https://dl.gi.de/items/afe196f3-4f7e-4158-90ef-89910ff1afaf/full)
- Knowledge loss: Feilkas, Ratiu and Jürgens (2009 industrial case study, three business information systems) found implicit architectural knowledge "especially volatile" in projects with high developer fluctuation. Rigby et al. (2016) quantified turnover-induced knowledge loss in Chrome and an Avaya project; Nassif and Robillard (ICSME 2017) revisited it and note a file's knowledge becomes inaccessible when its last knowledgeable editor leaves. Type: case studies and repository mining. Population: professionals. Strength: Moderate that loss happens; no evidence on how to prevent it by training. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Feilkas et al. 2009](https://teamscale.com/hubfs/26978363/Publications/2009-the-loss-of-architectural-knowledge-during-system-evolution-an-industrial-case-study.pdf?hsLang=en); [Rigby et al. 2016](https://researchr.org/publication/RigbyZDM16); [Nassif and Robillard 2017](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/icsme2017.pdf)
- AI assistance and comprehension (directly relevant): Shen and Tamkin (arXiv 2601.20245, January 2026), randomized experiment, 52 participants (26 per arm), mostly freelance or professional developers with over a year of Python, learning the unfamiliar Trio library. The AI-assisted group scored 4.15 points lower on a 27-point quiz taken without AI (about 17%), Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.010. No statistically significant speed gain. Six interaction patterns: three low-scoring (AI delegation, progressive reliance, iterative AI debugging; each n = 4; quiz under 40%) and three high-scoring (generation-then-comprehension n = 2, hybrid code-explanation n = 3, conceptual inquiry n = 7; quiz 65% or more). Authors' limits: one task, about one hour, chat interface (agentic tools may reduce engagement further), one quiz, patterns are associative with tiny cells. Type: randomized controlled study. Population: working developers (crowd-recruited), learning a new library. Strength: Moderate for "delegating reduces comprehension of new material"; Weak for the pattern analysis. `[READ]` — [Shen and Tamkin 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)

### Inferences
- The questions professionals find hardest (rationale, reachability, "what will this change affect") are the same ones a system-level prediction prompt would ask. So the prediction target is well chosen, but the same evidence says these questions are hard to *answer from the code*, so the tool's "what actually happened" side must be trustworthy or the feedback will teach wrong lessons.
- Because knowledge lives in heads and decays with disuse and with turnover, and because agent-written changes remove the editing that correlates with familiarity (Krüger), a plausible mechanism for loss of understanding under agent use exists. This is an inference: no study has measured system-level understanding in engineers over months of agent use.
- Code review already functions mainly as an understanding and knowledge-transfer activity. A predict-then-see step is closer to existing review practice than to a classroom exercise, which may help acceptance.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Letovsky (1987), Pennington (1987), Brooks (1983) or Soloway and Ehrlich (1984) primary sources this session. The commonly cited claims (Letovsky: programmers as opportunistic processors who ask why/how/what questions and form conjectures; Pennington: programmers first build a control-flow "program model", then a domain-level "situation model", and those who build the situation model comprehend better; Soloway and Ehrlich: experts use plans and are disrupted when code violates conventions) are from memory and unverified here. All were small studies on small programs.
- No study found that trains or measures *system-level* mental model accuracy (blast radius, affected parties, invariants) in experienced engineers longitudinally.
- No study found that measures forgetting of architecture knowledge with an objective test rather than self-rated familiarity.
- Shen and Tamkin tested learning of a *new library* over one hour. Whether the same loss occurs for understanding of one's *own system* under agent use is unknown.

## 2. Prediction before outcome

### Takeaway
Asking for a guess before showing the answer reliably improves memory for the specific thing asked about (around g = 0.5 in students), but benefits do not spread to material not asked about, are smaller for conceptual than factual questions, and require feedback. Evidence for professional judgment, as opposed to student recall, is nearly absent; the best professional evidence for "predict, then get scored" comes from forecasting tournaments (section 6), not from the pretesting literature.

### Cited Findings
- Prequestion effect, preregistered meta-analysis (St. Hilaire, Chan and Ahn, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review 2024): specific effect g = 0.54 (k = 97); general effect on non-prequestioned material g = 0.04 (k = 91). Bias-adjusted specific effect g = 0.45. Requiring a guess mattered most: g = 0.65 when participants generated an answer versus g = 0.22 when they only read the question (p < .001). Factual prequestions g = 0.58 versus conceptual g = 0.28 (p = .073, marginal). Adults g = 0.62 versus grade-school children g = 0.22. Lower prior knowledge (lower prequestion accuracy) tended to give a larger effect (β = −0.49, p = .094); the outliers with about 80% prequestion accuracy were "adult experts in their fields studying new information in their area of expertise". General effect was slightly positive for nontested questions related to the prequestions (g = 0.17) versus unrelated (g = 0.01). Authors: most studies used college undergraduates; few classroom or online studies; these designs give no corrective feedback at prediction time (learners find the answer in the later material). Type: meta-analysis. Population: mostly undergraduates. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength for this tool: Moderate for "predicting helps you retain the answer to what you predicted"; Weak for building general system skill. `[READ]` — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8) ([NSF open copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812))
- Brod (2021), theoretical review "Predicting as a learning strategy": no effect sizes. Predicting is framed for simple declarative learning, not complex conceptual change. It requires some familiarity so the learner can commit with some confidence; feedback is essential. Predicting produced a pupil-dilation surprise response to the correct answer that postdiction and example-generation did not; surprise scales with confidence, so low-confidence guesses give little. Predictions raised curiosity more than generating examples, but overall retention did not differ between those conditions in that study. Unknown per the author: whether predicting from a complex theory differs from predicting isolated facts. Type: narrative review. Population: children and university students. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Weak to Moderate (mechanism), Very weak for professionals. `[READ]` — [Brod 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8642250/)
- Pretesting can beat post-study retrieval practice in some conditions: Pan and Sana (2021) report a d = 0.30 advantage for pretesting over retrieval practice in one condition. Type: controlled experiments. Population: students/online adults. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Weak. `[SECONDARY]` (search summary of the paper) — [Pan and Sana 2021](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PS_2021.pdf)
- Generation effect meta-analysis (Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott and McDaniel 2007, Memory and Cognition): 445 effect sizes from 86 studies, 11 moderators. The commonly quoted overall d of about 0.40 was **not verified** this session (only a low-quality secondary source gave it). A larger update exists: McCurdy et al. 2020 (126 articles, 310 experiments, 1,653 estimates). Type: meta-analyses. Population: overwhelmingly students; mostly word-level materials. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Moderate (for recall). `[ABSTRACT]` for study counts; effect size unverified — [Bertsch et al. 2007, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/); [McCurdy et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3)
- Productive failure / problem solving before instruction (Sinha and Kapur 2021, Review of Educational Research): 53 studies, 166 comparisons; g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.51) in favour of problem-solving first; g = 0.37 to 0.58 with high fidelity to productive-failure design; publication-bias-adjusted estimate g = 0.87 (a sensitivity estimate). Reversed for second- to fifth-graders and for *domain-general skills*, where instruction-first was better. Type: meta-analysis. Population: school and university students, mostly maths and science. Strength: Moderate for conceptual knowledge in students; Weak for professionals. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Sinha and Kapur 2021](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1406031499744456704); [ETH comparative meta-analysis](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/492056)
- Hypercorrection: errors made with high confidence are more likely to be corrected after feedback than low-confidence errors (Butterfield and Metcalfe; reviewed in Metcalfe 2017, Annual Review of Psychology). Boundary: Butler, Fazio and Marsh (2011) found that on a delayed test high-confidence errors were *more likely to be reproduced* than low-confidence errors, and noted almost all studies used short delays; Butterfield and Mangels (2003) found the effect held at a delay. Durability is not settled. Type: controlled experiments and review. Population: students, general-knowledge facts. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Moderate short-term, Weak long-term. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Butler, Fazio and Marsh 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079415); [Metcalfe 2017](https://wesleyan.edu/ofcd/resources/OFCD%20Resource%20Docs/Learning%20from%20Errors.pdf)
- Predict-observe-explain (POE): a 2024 meta-analysis of 35 studies reports pooled g of about 0.98 (95% CI about 0.77 to 1.19) on science achievement. The underlying studies are mostly small quasi-experiments in school science from one research community; the size is likely inflated. Type: meta-analysis of low-quality studies. Population: school students. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Weak. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Koyunlu Ünlü 2024](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/4299696)
- Transfer of test-enhanced learning (Pan and Rickard 2018, Psychological Bulletin, 192 effect sizes): overall transfer d about 0.40, with *no positive transfer* when none of the three moderators (response congruency, elaborated retrieval practice, high initial test performance) is present. Type: meta-analysis. Population: mostly students. Strength: Moderate. `[SECONDARY]` for the number — [Pan and Rickard 2018](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)

### Inferences
- The clearest design implication from the meta-analysis: the benefit attaches to what was actually predicted, and requires a committed guess (0.65 versus 0.22). A prompt that asks "what will this touch?" should improve retention of what that particular change touched; it should not be expected, on this evidence alone, to improve understanding of parts of the system the prediction did not mention.
- Conceptual prequestions showed about half the effect of factual ones (marginal). System-level predictions are conceptual. Expect a smaller effect than the headline 0.54.
- Experts with high prior accuracy sat at the low-benefit end of the prior-knowledge moderator. For experienced engineers the *memory* benefit of predicting what they already know will be small; the value would have to come from the misses (surprise, hypercorrection) and from calibration, not from the routine hits.
- Surprise scales with confidence (Brod), and hypercorrection needs a confident error. This supports collecting a confidence rating with the prediction. But high-confidence errors may return after a delay (Butler et al.), which argues for revisiting misses later rather than relying on one-off correction.
- Productive failure reversed for domain-general skills. If the goal is a general "systems thinking" skill rather than knowledge of one system, the productive-failure literature gives no support.

### Gaps
- No study found testing the prequestion or pretesting effect in working professionals on judgment tasks (the meta-analysis mentions only a 1978 study with adult experts as outliers).
- No study found of predict-before-outcome for software change impact, in students or professionals.
- I could not verify the Bertsch et al. 2007 overall effect size in the source.
- Whether repeated prediction over months yields cumulative skill, rather than item-specific memory, is unknown in this literature. Nearly all studies are single sessions with retention intervals of minutes to a week.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) was not retrieved this session; its utility ratings (practice testing and distributed practice rated high; self-explanation and interleaving moderate) are quoted from memory and unverified here.

## 3. Diagrams

### Takeaway
Diagrams of software help comprehension modestly and conditionally: design-level diagrams that match the code help, and one good professional study shows better correctness of changes with UML, but professionals mostly use informal sketches and rarely keep formal diagrams. Constructing a diagram teaches more than studying one in student studies (concept maps g = 0.72 versus 0.43), yet learner drawing does not reliably beat a good provided illustration. There is no direct evidence on auto-generated "what changed" diagrams.

### Cited Findings
**Software diagrams**
- Dzidek, Arisholm and Briand (IEEE TSE 2008): controlled experiment with 20 professional developers maintaining a real non-trivial system. The UML group had a 54% increase in functional correctness of changes (p = 0.03) and an insignificant 7% improvement in design quality (p = 0.22). Described as the first such experiment with professionals on a real system. Type: controlled experiment. Population: professionals (n = 20). Strength: Moderate (single study, small n, but right population and task). `[ABSTRACT]` — [summary at It Will Never Work in Theory](https://neverworkintheory.org/2011/08/17/three-empirical-evaluations-of-uml.html); [Simula record](https://web-backend.simula.no/bibcite/export/bibtex/bibcite_reference/9114)
- Scanniello, Gravino and colleagues (EMSE 2018) pooled 12 controlled experiments on whether UML aids source-code comprehension. I could not open the full text (file too large); from memory, the result is that design-level diagrams help and analysis-level (requirements-phase) diagrams do not, and most participants were students. That summary is unverified here. Type: family of experiments/meta-analysis. Population: mostly students. Strength: Moderate. Effect sizes unverified — [EMSE 2018](https://www.iris.unisa.it/retrieve/e2915b35-290e-8981-e053-6605fe0a83a3/EMSE_2018_gravino_risi.pdf)
- Forward-designed versus reverse-engineered UML diagrams (2013, 40 Master's students): authors call results preliminary. Type: controlled experiment. Population: students. Strength: Weak. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/177140)
- A family of experiments on documenting design-pattern instances (88 participants including professionals): graphical UML class diagrams gave comprehension benefits similar to textual comments; professionals with documented pattern instances understood code significantly better than with code alone. Type: family of controlled experiments. Population: students and professionals. Strength: Moderate. Implication: the *information* mattered, not the graphical form. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Gravino et al.](https://www.academia.edu/22417252/Documenting_Design_Pattern_Instances_a_Family_of_Experiments_on_Source_Code_Comprehensibility)
- Petre, "UML in Practice" (ICSE 2013): interviews with 50 professional engineers in 50 companies; five patterns of use; most do not use UML, and those who do use it selectively and informally (as a "thought tool", for communication), in early design or implementation but rarely both. Type: interview study. Population: professionals. Strength: Moderate (descriptive). `[ABSTRACT]` — [ICSE 2013 page](https://2013.icse-conferences.org/content/uml-practice.html); [Open University follow-up](https://oro.open.ac.uk/41542)
- Baltes and Diehl, "Sketches and Diagrams in Practice" (FSE 2014): field study at three companies plus survey of 394 participants. Most sketches were informal; main purposes were designing, explaining and understanding; more than half were made on analog media. Type: survey. Population: professionals. Strength: Moderate (descriptive). `[ABSTRACT]` — [arXiv 1706.09172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.09172)
- CodeCity controlled experiment (Wettel, Lanza and Robbes, ICSE 2011): 41 participants from academia and industry across four sites; the city visualisation gave a statistically significant increase in task correctness and decrease in completion time versus the baseline tools. Type: controlled experiment. Population: mixed. Strength: Moderate for program-comprehension tasks with a purpose-built visualisation; magnitudes not read. `[ABSTRACT]` — [ICSE 2011 page](https://2011.icse-conferences.org/content/software-systems-cities-controlled-experiment.html); [PDF](https://www.inf.unibz.it/~rrobbes/p/ICSE2011-codecity.pdf)

**Layout**
- Ghoniem, Fekete and Castagliola (InfoVis 2004): for graphs larger than about twenty vertices, matrix views beat node-link diagrams on most of seven generic tasks; only path finding consistently favoured node-link. Later large online studies partly disagree: node-link better for memorability and connectivity tasks, and best understanding on 20- and 50-node social networks. Type: controlled experiments. Population: students/online adults. Strength: Moderate that node-link degrades with size and density; conflicting on the crossover. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Ghoniem et al. 2004](https://www.lisn.upsaclay.fr/publications/a-comparison-of-the-readability-of-graphs-using-node-link-and-matrix-based-representations/); contradicted in part by [Okoe et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.13716v2) and [Network Science online experiment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/network-science/article/understanding-nodelink-and-matrix-visualizations-of-networks-a-largescale-online-experiment/C293136C4761F09D4C2C328E2BC19287)
- Purchase's experiments: minimising edge crossings was "by far the most important aesthetic" for understanding graph structure; for UML diagrams, aesthetic priorities differ from domain-independent graphs and semantics may need to drive layout. Type: controlled experiments. Population: students. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Purchase 1997](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-63938-1_67.pdf); [Purchase et al. UML aesthetics](https://jgaa.info/index.php/jgaa/article/view/paper54)

**Learner-generated versus provided**
- Concept maps (Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano and Adesope 2018): 142 independent effect sizes, n = 11,814; overall g = 0.58; constructing maps g = 0.72 versus studying maps g = 0.43, each relative to its comparison condition. 118 of 142 studies were STEM. Type: meta-analysis. Population: students. STUDENT-RECALL (retention and comprehension tests). Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Schroeder et al. 2018](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)
- Learning by drawing (Fiorella and Zhang 2018 review): drawing looks beneficial compared with reading only or text-focused strategies; compared with other model-focused strategies or with *viewing instructor-provided illustrations*, effects are mixed and depend on guidance. Type: systematic review. Population: students. STUDENT-RECALL. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Fiorella and Zhang 2018](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2018/journal-article-abstracts/s10648-018-9444-8/)
- Drawing support meta-analysis (14 studies, 16 comparisons, N = 1,213): drawing support in general was not related to outcomes; support that did not facilitate integration gave g = −0.15 for comprehension. Earlier meta-analyses (Cromley et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021) also found supported drawing did not beat unsupported drawing. A 2025 dissertation found drawing combined with self-explaining hindered low-prior-knowledge students and helped high-prior-knowledge students. Type: meta-analysis; dissertation. Population: students. Strength: Weak to Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Educational Psychology Review 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10067-7); [Illinois dissertation](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/136594)

### Inferences
- For professionals, the weight of evidence is that the *content* of a diagram (accurate, design-level, matched to the code and the task) matters more than notation. Formal UML is not expected by practitioners; informal box-and-arrow is the native form.
- The "generate beats study" result (0.72 versus 0.43) and the "specific effect only" prequestion result point the same way: having the developer commit to their own picture of the change first (even as a list or rough sketch), then showing the tool's diagram for comparison, has better support than only showing a diagram. This is an inference across literatures, not a tested combination. And drawing versus a good provided picture is a wash in student studies, so the generation step should be cheap.
- Node-link diagrams degrade beyond a few tens of nodes and with edge crossings. A change diagram should stay small (the touched neighbourhood) rather than draw the system.
- The helpful finding with professionals (Dzidek) was on *correctness of changes*, the outcome this tool cares about, but used up-to-date human-made design documentation. An auto-drawn diagram of an agent's change is a different artefact; its accuracy and level of abstraction are untested.

### Gaps
- No study found on C4 diagrams' effect on comprehension. C4 appears to be supported by practitioner adoption and opinion only; I found no controlled evaluation.
- No study found on diagrams of *changes* (diff-level or impact diagrams) and their effect on comprehension or review quality.
- I did not retrieve Van Meter and Garner (2005), Schmeck et al. (2014), Leutner et al., or Fiorella and Mayer's generative learning review this session; their specific effect sizes are not quoted. The Cromley et al. 2020 pooled effect for drawing versus text-only was not retrieved.
- When diagrams mislead: I found no study quantifying harm from *inaccurate or stale* software diagrams, though stale documentation is the usual practitioner complaint. Evidence on diagrams misleading comes from general graph-readability work (crossings, density) rather than from software-specific error studies.
- No longitudinal evidence that viewing diagrams builds a durable mental model in engineers.

## 4. Self-explanation, explaining to others, comparison and contrasting cases

### Takeaway
Prompted self-explanation (g about 0.55) and comparing cases (d about 0.50) are among the better-supported generative strategies, but again in students on learning tests. They suggest the "explain the gap between your prediction and what happened" step is where learning is most likely, more than the prediction itself.

### Cited Findings
- Self-explanation meta-analysis (Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi and Winne 2018): 69 effect sizes from 64 reports; overall g = 0.55. Most studies involved undergraduates. Reported moderator values in a secondary table: interrogative prompts g = 0.56 (k = 36), imperative prompts g = 0.40 (k = 12). Type: meta-analysis. Population: mostly undergraduates. STUDENT-RECALL (learning tests, some transfer). Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` for 0.55; `[SECONDARY]` for moderators — [Clearing House Unterricht summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/CHU-KR-25_ENG_Bisra_2018_Selbsterklaerungen.pdf); [SFU record](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/34322)
- Case comparison meta-analysis (Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn 2013, Educational Psychologist): 57 experiments, 336 tests; d = 0.50 (95% CI 0.44 to 0.56) over traditional instruction and other case formats. Larger effects when learners were asked to find similarities, when a principle was provided *after* comparing, with perceptual content, and on immediate tests. Type: meta-analysis. Population: mostly students. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Alfieri et al. 2013](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/Schunn/papers/ContrastingCasesMeta-AlfieriEtAl2013.pdf)
- ICAP (Chi and Wylie 2014) predicts interactive > constructive > active > passive engagement. Critiques: Thurn and colleagues argue overt behaviour is a poor proxy for cognition and that practitioners should not rely on ICAP to choose interventions; the original authors note its predictions can oppose cognitive load theory and may fail in domains without deeper rationales. Type: theoretical framework with supporting review; commentary. Population: students. Strength: Weak as a design rule. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Chi and Wylie 2014](https://chilab.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf); [Thurn et al., npj Science of Learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10652002/)
- In Shen and Tamkin's experiment, the high-scoring AI users were those who asked conceptual questions or asked for explanations alongside code (65% to 86% on the quiz) versus delegators (24% to 39%); associative, tiny cells. Type: randomized study, exploratory sub-analysis. Population: working developers. Strength: Weak. `[READ]` — [Shen and Tamkin 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)
- Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025), field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school maths students: unrestricted GPT-4 access improved practice performance by 48% but reduced later unaided exam performance by 17% versus control; a tutor with guardrails (hints, no answers) improved practice by 127% and largely removed the harm, ending about equal to control on the exam. Students using the tutor believed they had done better than they had. Type: randomized field experiment. Population: high-school students. Strength: Moderate (for students). `[ABSTRACT]` — [PNAS record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2422633122); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)
- Kestin et al. (Scientific Reports 2025), randomized crossover, 194 Harvard physics undergraduates: a structured GPT-4 tutor beat in-class active learning, effect size 0.63 SD by linear regression (0.73 to 1.3 by quantile regression). Type: randomized crossover. Population: elite undergraduates, two weeks. Strength: Weak for our purpose. `[SECONDARY]` for numbers — [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- Lee et al. (CHI 2025), survey of 319 knowledge workers (936 task examples): higher confidence in the AI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking; higher self-confidence with more. Self-report, no causal design. Type: survey. Population: professionals (knowledge workers). Strength: Weak. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1135061)

### Inferences
- The Alfieri moderator "principle provided after comparing" fits a predict-compare-then-explain order: let the developer compare their prediction with the diagram first, then offer the tool's explanation of the difference.
- The AI studies agree on one thing across students and working developers: getting the answer handed over lowers later unaided performance, and structure that makes the person engage first removes most of that harm. They also show people misjudge their own learning (Bastani). This is the strongest recent support for the tool's premise, but none of it is about system-level understanding or about months.

### Gaps
- No evidence found on self-explanation or case comparison in working engineers.
- No study compares "explain the mismatch" with "just see the outcome" after a prediction, for professionals.
- "Explaining to others" (learning by teaching) was not searched specifically this session; no findings to report.

## 5. Building skill over time: spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice, deliberate practice, transfer

### Takeaway
Spaced, question-based practice with feedback is the best-evidenced method for durable change in working professionals: in clinicians it changed real behaviour for at least 72 weeks after a 36-week programme. Deliberate practice as usually measured explains very little of performance differences in professions (under 1% in one meta-analysis, itself disputed). Transfer beyond what was practised is limited and conditional.

### Cited Findings
- Kerfoot et al. (American Journal of Preventive Medicine 2010): multi-site RCT, 95 primary care clinicians (physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants; mean age about 49) at eight VA centres. Intervention: 36 emailed clinical scenario questions with immediate feedback and explanation, zero to two emails a week over 36 weeks, in four cycles that repeated content with changed clinical context. Inappropriate PSA screening: 10.5% versus 14.2% during the intervention (p = 0.041); 7.8% versus 13.1% over the 72 weeks after it ended (p = 0.011), a 40% relative reduction, with no significant erosion (interaction p = 0.63). Knowledge tests: 95% versus 76% at week 36 (effect sizes 1.1 to 1.2). Limits: one narrow behaviour; 95 of 260 eligible enrolled; performance plateaued after cycle 2; authors flag fatigue as a concern; lead author holds equity in the company commercialising the method. Type: RCT. Population: practising clinicians. Strength: Strong for narrow factual-rule behaviour; Moderate as analogy for engineers. `[READ]` — [Kerfoot et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2994103)
- Counter-example: a trial of a similar spaced-education programme with Australian general practitioners found "no evidence" of an impact on management of standardised patients; not all participants completed it. Type: RCT. Population: practising GPs. `[ABSTRACT]` — [JMIR 2014](https://www.jmir.org/2014/2/e63)
- Spaced education in 724 urology residents was retained much better over 29 weeks than web modules. Type: RCT (as described by the vendor/author interview). Population: residents. `[SECONDARY]` — [Qstream Q&A with Kerfoot](https://qstream.com/blog/qa-with-b-price-kerfoot-md-edm-how-long-do-the-benefits-of-qstream-last)
- Systematic review of distributed practice and retrieval practice in health professions education (Trumble et al. 2023): both improve academic grades; only 5 studies compared spacing schedules (expanding, equal, contracting); time on task was often unreported. Type: systematic review. Population: health professions students mainly. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Trumble et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11078833/)
- Test-enhanced learning in continuing medical education: a CME study randomised 75 physicians (49 completed); the paper notes earlier CME results were inconsistent, that Larsen et al. found weekly quizzes beat repeated study at five and a half months in residents, and that "practicing clinicians have an aversion towards taking tests", which causes dropout. Type: RCT and review. Population: practising physicians and residents. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Feldman et al., PMC6104310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6104310); [review of testing in physician learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703)
- Interleaving meta-analysis (Brunmair and Richter 2019, Psychological Bulletin): overall g = 0.42; maths tasks g = 0.34; for word materials *blocking* was better (g = −0.39). Effect depends on similarity between categories. Type: meta-analysis. Population: mostly students; inductive category learning. STUDENT-RECALL/classification. Strength: Weak for this tool. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Brunmair and Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)
- Deliberate practice (Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald 2014, Psychological Science; 88 studies, over 11,000 participants): variance in performance explained was 26% for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and less than 1% for professions; about 12% overall. The professions category was small (one press account says four papers, including programming). Disputed: Ericsson and Harwell's reanalysis with stricter definitions of deliberate practice reports 29% to 61% of variance after correction. Type: meta-analysis and rebuttal. Population: includes professionals. Strength: Moderate that hours of self-reported practice do not predict professional performance; unsettled for properly designed practice. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Macnamara et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24986855/); disputed by [Ericsson and Harwell 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6824411)
- Transfer: retrieval practice transfers only under specific conditions (Pan and Rickard 2018; see section 2). The prequestion general effect is g = 0.04 (section 2). Productive failure reverses for domain-general skills (section 2).

### Inferences
- Kerfoot's design is the closest working template: short scenario questions with immediate explanation, at most two a week, cycled with changed context, over months, to busy mid-career professionals. It changed practice, not only test scores. Differences that limit the analogy: the clinical target was a simple rule with a known right answer; a system-level prediction has a fuzzier right answer and the content changes with each change.
- "Repeat the same content with changed context" (Kerfoot's cycles) maps to re-asking about the same subsystem or invariant when a later change touches it. A tool tied to real changes gets spacing and varied context naturally but cannot control the schedule.
- Given the transfer findings, months of predictions will most plausibly build knowledge of *this* system. A claim that it builds a general, portable system-design skill has no direct support.
- The clinician test-aversion finding predicts dropout if the prompt feels like a quiz.

### Gaps
- No randomized study found of spaced or retrieval practice in software engineers or other corporate employees (only vendor case studies without controls).
- No evidence found on the optimal spacing or frequency of prompts for professionals; Kerfoot explicitly says optimal spacing and length were not established.
- No evidence found on deliberate practice of system-level reasoning in engineering.
- The "professions under 1%" figure rests on few studies; I did not read the domain table in the paper.

## 6. Calibration

### Takeaway
Probability training plus frequent scored feedback improved forecasting accuracy by about 10% and reduced overconfidence in a multi-year tournament with thousands of adult volunteers; this is the best evidence that "predict, get scored, repeat" improves judgment in adults. In medicine, confidence tracks accuracy poorly on hard cases and feedback helps calibration mainly on easy ones.

### Cited Findings
- Good Judgment Project (Tetlock, Mellers, Rohrbaugh and Chen 2014, Current Directions in Psychological Science): forecasters were randomly assigned in factorial designs. Cognitive-debiasing training accounted for "about a 10% advantage of the training condition over the no-training condition"; teaming and prediction markets a "roughly 10% boost" over working alone; aggregation algorithms an additional 35% over unweighted averaging; the top 2% each year were formed into "super-forecaster" teams. The tournament scored over 200 questions with Brier scores. Type: randomized field experiments within a tournament; this source is a review by the authors. Population: adult volunteers, many educated professionals, not selected domain experts. Strength: Moderate to Strong for forecasting discrete, resolvable events. `[READ]` — [Tetlock et al. 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/2014---forecasting-tournaments-tools-for-increasing-transparency-and-improving-debate.pdf)
- Mellers et al. (Psychological Science 2014): probability training (correcting biases, using reference classes, averaging estimates), teaming and tracking "improved both calibration and resolution". Exact Brier figures not read. Type: randomized experiments. Population: as above. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Mellers et al. 2014 record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2131472525)
- Moore et al. (Management Science 2017): 494,552 forecasts by 2,860 forecasters over three years. Small overconfidence overall (about 3%); training and teams together reduced it to about 1%; confidence rose at about the same rate as accuracy and good calibration persisted. Type: analysis of tournament data with randomized conditions. Population: adult volunteers. Strength: Moderate to Strong. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Moore et al. 2017](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1700r340)
- Meyer et al. (JAMA Internal Medicine 2013), vignette study of physicians: calibration was worse on difficult cases (p < .001) with overconfidence; higher confidence was related to fewer requests for additional tests (p = .01). Type: controlled vignette study. Population: practising physicians. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/sms/resource/pt/mdl-23979070)
- Feedback on calibration in medicine: feedback on prior diagnostic performance improved calibration for students and experts in some studies (Nederhand et al.), improved interns' calibration, but in general-practice residents did not improve calibration on more difficult cases. Type: controlled studies. Population: medical students, residents, physicians. Strength: Weak to Moderate, mixed. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Advances in Health Sciences Education 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10459-021-10080-9); [PMC8938348](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8938348)

### Inferences
- Forecasting gains came with conditions the tool can partly copy: questions that resolve unambiguously, a proper score, quick and frequent feedback, short training in base rates and reference classes. A system-level prediction ("which components will change?") can be made resolvable and scored; "who is affected" and "what must stay true" resolve less cleanly, and unresolvable predictions cannot train calibration.
- Overconfidence is worst on hard cases, and high confidence suppresses checking (Meyer). For engineers this suggests the value of a confidence rating is highest on unfamiliar parts of the system, where a confident wrong prediction should trigger more review.
- The forecasters were general adult volunteers predicting many independent events. Whether calibration learned on change-impact predictions carries over to other engineering judgments is untested.

### Gaps
- No study found on calibration training for software engineers' predictions of change impact. (Software estimation overconfidence literature exists but was not searched this session.)
- I did not verify exact Brier-score improvements from Mellers et al. 2014; the 10% figure is from the Tetlock et al. summary.
- No meta-analysis of calibration training was retrieved. Lichtenstein and Fischhoff's classic calibration-training studies were not retrieved; unverified here.
- Proper scoring rules: evidence that the *choice* of scoring rule (Brier versus log) matters for learning was not searched; no findings.

## 7. Expertise reversal and fading of support

### Takeaway
Guidance that helps novices can slow or annoy experts; a 2025 meta-analysis confirms the effect and finds it stronger in older learners and higher education. I could not read the pooled effect size. The practical implication is to make support for experienced engineers optional, minimal and fast, but there is little direct evidence on what experienced professionals find patronising.

### Cited Findings
- Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters and Brod (2025), "A cornerstone of adaptivity: a meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect", Learning and Instruction 98, 102142: 60 studies, 176 effect sizes, almost 6,000 participants. The effect grows with learner age, is strongest in higher education, varies by discipline and is weaker in language and humanities. Pooled effect size **not verified** (only blog summaries reached). Type: meta-analysis. Population: school and university learners; "experts" here means higher prior knowledge, usually still students. Strength: Moderate for the phenomenon; Weak as applied to 10-year engineers. `[SECONDARY]` — [Learning and the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/); [bibliographic record](https://loterre.istex.fr/P66/fr/page/-R5C8JXLW-X?clang=en)
- Related signals from sources above: the prequestion effect tended to be smaller with higher prior knowledge, with adult experts as the high-accuracy outliers (St. Hilaire et al. 2024, `[READ]`, [source](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8)); drawing plus self-explaining hurt low-prior-knowledge students and helped high-prior-knowledge ones ([Illinois dissertation](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/136594), `[ABSTRACT]`); practising clinicians "have an aversion towards taking tests" ([PMC6104310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6104310), `[ABSTRACT]`).
- An experimental test of ICAP reported findings that challenge it and suggested boundary conditions of task complexity and learner preparedness. Type: controlled study. `[ABSTRACT]` — [University of Georgia record](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/27487)

### Inferences
- Two effects pull in different directions for experts. Generative demands (predicting, drawing, explaining) tend to suit high-prior-knowledge learners better than novices, so prediction is a reasonable fit for experienced engineers. But instructional guidance (worked explanations, scaffolds, hints) is where reversal bites, so the feedback side should be terse and expandable rather than tutorial.
- Since routine correct predictions carry little learning for experts, a tool that asks on every change will mostly ask experts things they already know. Selecting prompts for cases where the prediction is likely to be informative (unfamiliar area, cross-boundary change, past miss) is consistent with the evidence, though untested.

### Gaps
- Kalyuga's (2007) review and the pooled effect in Tetzlaff et al. were not read; no effect size is quoted.
- I found no study of what experienced professionals find patronising in learning prompts, beyond the clinician test-aversion remark. This is an evidence gap, not a known result.
- "Expert" in this literature almost always means a better-prepared student. Nothing here was measured on engineers with years of experience.

## 8. Habituation to prompts and interruption cost

### Takeaway
Repeated identical prompts are tuned out within a session and across weeks; varying their appearance slows this, and withholding them lets attention recover. In clinical systems most alerts are overridden. Interruptions to programming are costly to resume from. Together these argue for rare, varied prompts placed at natural boundaries; there is no evidence giving a specific safe frequency.

### Cited Findings
- Vance, Jenkins, Anderson and colleagues (MIS Quarterly 2018; CHI 2017): five-day fMRI and eye-tracking lab study plus a three-week field study of mobile permission warnings. Neural activity and eye fixations fell rapidly with repetition within a session; partial recovery occurred from day to day when warnings were withheld; polymorphic (changing-appearance) warnings resisted habituation through the five days. In the field study, after 15 days adherence was 76% with changing warnings versus 55% with static ones. Type: controlled lab and field experiments. Population: adults/students. Strength: Moderate (security warnings, not learning prompts). `[ABSTRACT]`; 76% versus 55% is `[SECONDARY]` (press coverage) — [MISQ article](https://misq.org/tuning-out-security-warnings-a-longitudinal-examination-of-habituation-through-fmri-eye-tracking-and-field-experiments.html); [coverage](https://boingboing.net/2018/06/15/attentional-tragic-commons.html)
- Clinical decision support: a systematic review of 23 articles reports average alert override rates from 46.2% to 96.2%; a 2025 preprint meta-analysis of 44 studies pools drug-drug-interaction overrides at 79.8% (95% CI 73.0 to 86.7); only 12% of those studies assessed clinical outcomes. A review of systematic reviews found only one article gave an operational definition of "alert fatigue". Type: systematic reviews. Population: practising clinicians. Strength: Strong that professionals override most frequent, low-specificity alerts; Weak on the cause. `[ABSTRACT]` — [JMIR Medical Informatics 2020](https://medinform.jmir.org/2020/7/e15653/); [O'Sullivan et al. preprint](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/88578); [Ray, Wilson et al.](https://www.metrohealth.org/globalassets/metrohealth-documents/population-health-research-institute/ray-wilson-et-al-2026-alert-fatigue-systematic-review.pdf)
- Parnin and Rugaber (Software Quality Journal 2011): 10,000 recorded programming sessions from about 85 programmers plus a survey of 414. After an interruption, only about 10% of sessions resumed coding in under a minute; only 7% involved no navigation to other locations before editing. Type: observational log analysis and survey. Population: professional programmers. Strength: Moderate. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Parnin and Rugaber](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/reverse/repository/resumptionstrategies.pdf)
- Mark, Gudith and Klocke (CHI 2008): interrupted participants finished faster but reported more stress, frustration, effort and time pressure. The widely quoted "23 minutes 15 seconds to resume" figure has unclear provenance (sources disagree on which paper it comes from) and includes time spent on other work; do not use it as a cost estimate. Type: lab experiment; field observation. Population: office workers. Strength: Weak for a number. `[SECONDARY]` — [discussion of provenance](https://addyo.substack.com/p/it-takes-23-mins-to-recover-after)
- Kerfoot's effective schedule in clinicians was zero to two emails per week, and the authors still flagged fatigue (section 5, `[READ]`, [source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2994103)).

### Inferences
- A prediction prompt before an agent run sits at a task boundary the developer chose, which is the least costly place to interrupt; a prompt mid-task would incur the resumption costs Parnin and Rugaber measured. This placement is supported by inference only.
- "How rare" has one anchor from a successful professional programme: up to two short items a week. Asking on every agent change would be one to two orders of magnitude more frequent than anything shown to work, and matches the conditions under which clinicians override most alerts.
- "How varied": the polymorphic-warning result supports varying the form of the prompt, and Kerfoot's cycles support varying the context of the same content. Both are from other domains.

### Gaps
- No study found on habituation to *learning or reflection prompts* in developer tools, or on prompt frequency versus long-term engagement in professionals.
- No evidence found for a specific threshold frequency. Any number chosen will be a design guess to be tested.
- Habituation studies measure attention and adherence over days to weeks, not months.

## 9. Feedback: timing and form that help judgment; when intuition can be trusted

### Takeaway
Feedback improves performance on average (d about 0.41) but lowers it in over a third of cases, especially when it directs attention to the self rather than the task. Intuitive judgment becomes trustworthy only in regular environments with prompt, accurate feedback; a software system offers that for some questions (what a change touched) and not for others (long-delayed consequences).

### Cited Findings
- Kluger and DeNisi (1996, Psychological Bulletin): 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations; feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = 0.41) but over one third decreased performance; effectiveness falls as attention moves "closer to the self and away from the task". Type: meta-analysis. Population: mixed, includes workplace studies. Strength: Moderate to Strong. `[ABSTRACT]` — [Kluger and DeNisi abstract](https://scholars.huji.ac.il/node/1802)
- Kahneman and Klein (2009, American Psychologist): skilled intuition requires an environment regular enough to be predictable and an opportunity to learn those regularities through prolonged practice; secondary accounts quote the conclusion that it "depends essentially on the quality and speed of feedback, as well as sufficient opportunity to practice". Subjective confidence is not a reliable indicator of accuracy. Type: expert consensus review. Population: professionals across fields. Strength: Moderate (conceptual, widely accepted). Exact wording `[SECONDARY]` — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19739881/); [McKinsey interview with both authors](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategic-decisions-when-can-you-trust-your-gut)
- Feedback is essential for prediction to help: Brod (2021) notes guessing benefits only when the correct answer appears shortly after the guess (`[READ]`, [source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8642250/)). Kerfoot's items gave immediate feedback with an explanation (`[READ]`, [source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2994103)).
- People misjudge their own learning with AI help: students with the guarded GPT tutor believed they had performed significantly better although exam scores only matched control (Bastani et al., `[ABSTRACT]`, [source](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)).

### Inferences
- The diagram of "what actually happened" is task-level feedback in Kluger and DeNisi's terms. Scores, streaks, rankings or anything that reads as a judgment of the engineer moves attention toward the self and falls in the zone where feedback more often harms. This also bears on what experts find patronising.
- By Kahneman and Klein's criteria, "which parts did this change touch" is a high-validity question with immediate feedback, so intuition for it can be trained. "Who is affected" and "what must stay true" get true feedback only when something breaks, often weeks later and often not attributed. The tool's diagram can show *structural* reach promptly; it cannot show real downstream consequences, so feedback on those predictions will be a proxy. Training on a proxy risks confident but wrong intuition.
- Timing: the prediction literature and the clinician programme both used feedback right after the response. An agent run that takes minutes introduces a delay; I found no evidence on whether a delay of minutes matters for this kind of judgment.

### Gaps
- Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie (2020) feedback meta-analysis was not retrieved; not quoted.
- No evidence found on feedback form (diagram versus text versus diff) for improving professional judgment.
- The immediate-versus-delayed feedback literature was not searched; no claim made.
- Kahneman and Klein full text not read; the conditions are stated from the abstract record and secondary accounts.

## Cross-cutting summary for the report writer

### What the evidence supports, by strength (as applied to experienced engineers and system-level understanding)

| Claim | Strength | Main basis | Population caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handing over the answer (AI delegation) lowers later unaided comprehension; engaging first protects it | Moderate | Shen and Tamkin 2026 (d = 0.738, read); Bastani 2025 | One-hour task on a new library; high-school maths |
| Committing to a guess before the answer improves retention of the thing guessed | Moderate | St. Hilaire 2024 (g = 0.54; 0.65 with a generated guess; read) | Undergraduates, recall and comprehension tests; smaller for conceptual questions (0.28) |
| That benefit does not spread to things not predicted | Moderate | Same (g = 0.04; read) | Same |
| Scored predictions with feedback and brief training improve accuracy and calibration over years | Moderate to Strong | Good Judgment Project (about 10% from training; read) | Adult volunteers forecasting discrete events |
| Spaced scenario questions with immediate feedback change professional behaviour durably | Strong (narrow) | Kerfoot 2010 RCT (7.8% vs 13.1% over 72 weeks; read) | Clinicians, one simple rule; a similar GP trial found no effect |
| Accurate design-level diagrams improve correctness of changes | Moderate | Dzidek 2008 (54% more correct, n = 20 professionals) | Single study; human-made documentation |
| Constructing a representation beats studying one | Moderate | Schroeder 2018 (0.72 vs 0.43) | Students; drawing vs good provided picture is mixed |
| Frequent identical prompts get tuned out or overridden | Strong (other domains) | Alert override reviews; Vance et al. | Clinical alerts and security warnings, not learning prompts |
| Guidance suited to novices hampers the more knowledgeable | Moderate | Tetzlaff 2025 (effect size unverified) | "Experts" are mostly advanced students |
| Feedback helps on average, harms in a third of cases, worst when self-focused | Moderate to Strong | Kluger and DeNisi 1996 (d = 0.41) | Mixed settings |
| Months of this practice build a portable system-level skill | Very weak | No direct evidence; transfer findings are discouraging | — |

### What is unknown (state plainly)
- Whether predict-then-see works at all for system-level judgment in experienced engineers. No study exists in any population on predicting software change impact as a learning intervention.
- Whether effects accumulate over months. Almost all prediction, generation and drawing studies are single-session with short retention intervals.
- Whether gains transfer beyond the specific system and the specific things predicted. Available transfer evidence (g = 0.04 general prequestion effect; conditional retrieval transfer; reversal of productive failure for domain-general skills) suggests limited transfer.
- Whether auto-generated diagrams of an agent's change aid comprehension, and how much inaccurate diagrams harm it.
- What prompt frequency experienced engineers will tolerate; the only successful professional anchor is zero to two short items a week.
- Whether calibration trained on change-impact predictions generalises.
- What experts find patronising: no direct studies found.
