# The comprehension problem when coding agents write the code (evidence 2023 to Nov 2026)

Conventions used in every finding:

- **Type**: controlled study, observational data, survey, incident, opinion.
- **Tool class**: agent, chat, autocomplete, mixed.
- **Pop.**: students, working engineers, mixed.
- **Sells AI tools?**: whether the source has a commercial interest in AI coding or in tools that review or measure AI code.
- **Strength**: strong, moderate, weak (my rating of how far the claim can be relied on for the question at hand).
- **Verified**: "read" means I fetched the primary page this session and the figure was in it. "Secondary" means the figure came only from a search summary or a third-party write-up, so treat as unverified. "Not fetched" means I cite it from prior knowledge, did not open it this session, and give no figures as fact.

## 1. What do controlled studies and field data show about developers' understanding of AI-written or agent-written code and changes?

### Takeaway
Two randomised experiments (one with mostly junior working engineers using a chat assistant, one with CS students using an actual coding agent) both find that people who let the AI write the code finish as fast or faster yet score clearly lower on comprehension of what was built, with effect sizes of about d = 0.7 to 0.8. No study I found measures comprehension by experienced engineers using agents on a real, large codebase, and none measures understanding at system level (what else a change touches).

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic RCT (Shen and Tamkin, arXiv 2601.20245, Jan 2026). 52 software engineers, "mostly junior", weekly Python users, learning the unfamiliar Trio async library. AI group averaged 50% on a follow-up quiz against 67% for hand-coding (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.01). Largest gap on debugging questions. AI group was about two minutes faster, not significant (p = 0.391). Type: controlled study. Tool class: chat sidebar assistant with code access, NOT an agent (authors say they expect agentic products such as Claude Code to have more pronounced effects, footnote 1). Pop.: working engineers, mostly junior. Sells AI tools: yes (Anthropic), although the result runs against commercial interest. Strength: moderate (randomised, but small, one short task, comprehension tested minutes later). Verified: read. — [Anthropic research page](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- Same study, interaction patterns (qualitative, not causal). Low scorers (quiz average under 40%): AI delegation (n=4, 39%), progressive AI reliance (n=4, 35%), iterative AI debugging (n=4, 24%). High scorers (65% or more): generation-then-comprehension (n=2, 86%), hybrid code-explanation (n=3, 68%), conceptual inquiry (n=7, 65%). Strength: weak (cells of 2 to 7 people). Verified: read. — [Anthropic research page](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- "(Im)Paired Programming: Coding Agents Improve Productivity but Harm Understanding" (Balepur et al., Maryland, NYU, CMU; arXiv 2607.26375, 29 Jul 2026). 59 recruited, 54 analysed, CS students at two US colleges (17 BS, 31 MS, 11 PhD; 5.4 years coding experience on average). Agent condition: Aider with GPT-4.1, proposing diffs to accept or reject. Control: a chatbot with no code access. Task: build a 5x5 tic-tac-toe variant in 50 minutes, then extend it for 20 minutes with chatbot only. Agent users were faster and more accurate on the initial task but scored substantially lower on comprehension questions about their own code (p < 0.002, Cohen's d > 0.80). Extension-task accuracy was not much higher for agent users. Agent users sent 5.8 prompts on average against 11.6. Self-report (1 to 5): agent rated more helpful (4.7 vs 3.3) but self-rated understanding of own code lower (3.3 vs 4.2); most still preferred the agent. Type: controlled study. Tool class: agent. Pop.: students. Sells AI tools: no (academic). Strength: moderate (the only randomised agent-versus-non-agent comprehension experiment I found; students, toy web app, LLM-generated and LLM-scored quiz, preprint). Verified: read (first 100k characters of the HTML; exact time and accuracy means are only in a figure). — [arXiv 2607.26375](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26375v1)
- Same study, review behaviour inside the agent condition (exploratory, n=27): participants who auto-accepted edits had the lowest mean comprehension (0.615, n=6); those who accepted each file individually had the highest (0.777, n=7). Copy-pasting task criteria as the prompt had the lowest comprehension (0.654, n=25 across conditions); rewriting criteria into code-like syntax the highest (0.743, n=9). Less code, fewer comments and lower volume in the agent's JavaScript went with higher comprehension. Strength: weak (tiny cells, correlational). Verified: read. — [arXiv 2607.26375](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26375v1)
- METR early-2025 RCT is often cited on comprehension but measured time, not understanding. 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real issues on repositories averaging 22k+ stars and 1M+ lines, AI allowed or disallowed per issue (mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet; chat, agent mode and autocomplete all in use). Developers forecast a 24% speedup, afterwards believed they had been 20% faster, and were measured 19% slower. The relevant point for this question is the perception gap: experienced engineers misjudged the effect of AI on their own work by roughly 40 points. Type: controlled study. Tool class: mixed. Pop.: experienced working engineers. Sells AI tools: no (METR is a non-profit evaluator). Strength: strong for the perception gap in that setting, weak as evidence about comprehension. Verified: read. — [METR, 10 Jul 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- Field observation of professionals with agents (Huang et al., UC San Diego and others, arXiv 2512.14012, Dec 2025): 13 observed developers (3 to 25 years' experience) plus 99 survey respondents. Six observed participants stressed that human codebase understanding is necessary for production review; one (P7) objected to agents changing things "without me actually understanding what's going on"; eight said agents did more than asked (extra files, unneeded packages). No comprehension was measured. Type: observational data plus survey. Tool class: agent. Pop.: working engineers. Sells AI tools: no. Strength: weak for comprehension (qualitative, self-selected, single 45-minute sessions). Verified: read. — [arXiv 2512.14012](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14012)
- Essay-writing analogue, not coding: Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab, arXiv 2506.08872), 54 participants, EEG; LLM users showed the weakest brain connectivity, lowest sense of ownership and struggled to quote their own essays. Type: controlled study. Pop.: not developers. Strength: weak for this question (different task, small, 18 in the crossover session). Verified: read (abstract page). — [arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)

### Inferences
- The two randomised experiments agree in direction and size despite different populations and tool classes, which makes "delegating generation lowers immediate comprehension of the result" the best-supported claim in this whole area. It is still supported only for small, new, self-contained tasks.
- In both studies the comprehension loss was not uniform: it tracked how the person interacted (auto-accept and pure delegation lowest; asking conceptual questions, or generating then interrogating, highest). That is the main empirical hook for a comprehension tool: the same agent output can be understood or not depending on what the human does after generation.
- Self-rated understanding in the agent study fell (3.3 vs 4.2) while preference for the agent stayed high. People notice they understand less and accept the trade.
- The METR perception gap suggests developers' own reports of what AI did for them are unreliable, which weakens every survey in the sections below.

### Gaps
- No controlled study measures comprehension by experienced engineers using an agent on a large existing codebase. Untested.
- No study I found tests "awareness of what changed" directly (for example asking a developer to list the files, behaviours or invariants an agent's change touched). Untested.
- Comprehension was measured minutes after the task in both experiments. Retention over days or weeks is unknown.
- The agent study's quiz was tied to specific snippets (recall and reasoning about HTML, CSS and JS fragments), not system-level understanding. System-level comprehension of agent changes has not been measured anywhere I could find.
- A 21-participant "AIRELI" study on agent reliance and comprehension is mentioned by a vendor blog; I could not find a primary source. Unverified. — [explyt.ai blog](https://explyt.ai/en/blog/comprehension-debt-quiz-gate)
- "Programmers Are Poor and Overconfident Judges of LLM-Generated Assertions" (arXiv 2607.08885) surfaced in search and looks relevant, but I did not read it. — [arXiv 2607.08885](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.08885)

## 2. What is known about review of AI-written code: how much is reviewed, how well, review time, acceptance without reading, automation bias and over-trust?

### Takeaway
Surveys consistently show a gap between stated distrust and actual checking (Sonar: 96% do not fully trust AI code, 48% say they always verify before commit), and vendor telemetry shows review queues swelling as AI adoption rises, but almost all of this is self-report or vendor data. How carefully agent-written changes are actually read, and how many defects reviewers miss, has barely been measured.

### Cited Findings
- Sonar 2026 State of Code Developer Survey (announced 8 Jan 2026), "over 1,100 developers globally": 96% do not fully trust that AI-generated code is functionally correct; only 48% say they always check AI-assisted code before committing; AI said to account for 42% of committed code; 38% say reviewing AI code takes more effort than reviewing colleagues' code; 64% have started using autonomous agents; 35% use AI tools through personal accounts; top-cited negative is code that "looks correct but isn't reliable" (53%). Type: survey. Tool class: mixed. Pop.: working engineers. Sells AI tools: Sonar sells code verification and review products, so it benefits from a "verification gap" finding. Strength: weak to moderate (self-report, vendor, sampling method not on the page). Verified: read. — [Sonar press release](https://www.sonarsource.com/company/press-releases/sonar-data-reveals-critical-verification-gap-in-ai-coding/); vendor caveat noted by [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/09/devs_ai_code/)
- Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025: more developers distrust the accuracy of AI tools (46%) than trust it (33%), about 3% "highly trust"; 66% cite "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite" as top frustration; 45% say debugging AI-generated code takes more time; 87% are concerned about the accuracy of AI agent output. Type: survey. Pop.: mixed, mostly working developers. Sells AI tools: Stack Overflow sells data and AI products; modest interest. Strength: moderate as attitude data. Verified: secondary (search summaries of the Stack Overflow blog and press coverage; sources differ slightly, for example 29% vs 33% trust, 64% vs 66%). — [Stack Overflow blog](https://stackoverflow.blog/2025/12/29/developers-remain-willing-but-reluctant-to-use-ai-the-2025-developer-survey-results-are-here/); [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/stack-overflow-data-reveals-the-hidden-productivity-tax-of-almost-right-ai-code)
- Professionals observed using agents: 9 of 13 (69%) reviewed every agentic change, through diffs, test output and version control. In the survey of 99, "one-shotting code without verification" was rated unsuitable by 23 against 5 suitable. Respondents modified agent output about half the time (mean 3.0 on a 1 to 5 scale). Type: observational plus survey. Tool class: agent. Pop.: experienced working engineers (3+ years). Sells AI tools: no. Strength: weak to moderate (small, observed people behave better, authors note the survey likely over-selects AI-positive developers). Verified: read. — [arXiv 2512.14012](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14012)
- Student agent experiment: 6 of 27 agent users auto-accepted edits and had the lowest comprehension (see section 1). This is the only measured link I found between acceptance-without-reading and comprehension. Strength: weak. Verified: read. — [arXiv 2607.26375](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26375v1)
- Faros AI "Acceleration Whiplash" (AI Engineering Report Q2 2026), telemetry from 22K developers and 4K teams over two years, high versus low AI adoption within the same organisations: PR size +51%, bugs per PR about +28%, bugs per developer +54%, median review time "5X", incidents per PR about 3X (+242.7%), code churn about 10X, task throughput +33.7%, deployments per week -11.7%; 25% of PRs are reviewed by an AI agent. Type: observational data. Tool class: mixed. Pop.: working engineers. Sells AI tools: Faros sells engineering analytics and AI-governance products. Strength: weak to moderate (large, real telemetry, but correlational, baselines unstated, methodology behind a download gate). Verified: read (landing page only). — [Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/research/ai-acceleration-whiplash)
- Faros's earlier 2025 "AI Productivity Paradox" report is widely quoted as showing PR review time up 91%, PR size up 154% and bugs per developer up 9% on high-adoption teams (10,000+ developers, 1,255 teams). Verified: secondary only; do not mix with the 2026 figures. — [Faros AI 2025 report](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-productivity-paradox-report-2025)
- Agent-authored pull requests on GitHub (AIDev dataset, about 33k PRs from five agents in repos with 100+ stars; MSR 2026 papers): documentation, CI and build PRs are merged at higher rates, bug-fix and performance PRs at the lowest. One summary reports that 90.6% of PRs in the dataset have zero recorded review comments and acceptance rates from 0.83 (Codex) to 0.45 (Copilot). Type: observational data. Tool class: agent. Pop.: open-source maintainers. Sells AI tools: no. Strength: moderate for merge patterns; the zero-comment and per-agent figures are secondary (not on the abstract page I read) and zero comments does not prove no reading. — [arXiv 2601.15195](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15195v1); [arXiv 2602.02345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02345)
- METR 2025: the perception gap (expected +24%, believed +20%, measured -19%) is direct evidence of over-estimating AI's benefit among experienced engineers. Verified: read. — [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- DORA 2025 (Google): trust in AI output remains low while use is near universal; secondary coverage gives conflicting figures (for example 24% "a lot" or "a great deal" of trust and 30% "a little" or none). Type: survey. Sells AI tools: Google does. Strength: moderate. Verified: secondary. — [DORA 2025 PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf); [InfoQ summary](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/09/dora-state-of-ai-in-dev-2025)
- Older controlled evidence on over-trust, not fetched this session: Perry et al. (Stanford, CCS 2023) reported that participants with an AI assistant wrote less secure code while being more confident it was secure. Type: controlled study. Tool class: autocomplete/chat. Pop.: mostly students. Strength: moderate. Verified: not fetched, no figures given. — [arXiv 2211.03622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622)

### Inferences
- Stated behaviour is "distrust but do not always verify". The 48% "always check" figure is self-reported, so real checking is plausibly lower; nobody has instrumented it.
- Review is becoming the bottleneck: larger PRs, more of them, longer queues (Faros), and reviewers reporting more effort per review (Sonar). Larger diffs with the same reviewer attention means less attention per line, which is the mechanism by which comprehension would fall even if everyone "reviews".
- Experienced engineers observed in the field do review agent output, mostly at the level of diffs and tests. A diff shows lines changed, not system effect, so "reviewed every change" is compatible with not understanding what the change does to the system.

### Gaps
- No telemetry study I found reports the share of agent-generated changes accepted without being opened or read, time spent per line of agent diff, or defect-detection rates when reviewing agent code versus human code. Unknown.
- No controlled study of automation bias specific to coding agents (for example whether a confident agent summary reduces scrutiny of its diff). Untested as far as I found.
- Whether AI code review agents (25% of PRs in Faros data) raise or lower human reviewers' understanding is unknown. Papers such as arXiv 2607.13196 and 2607.21997 surfaced but were not read.
- CodeRabbit's widely quoted "AI PRs have about 1.7x more issues" report was not fetched; unverified.

## 3. What is "comprehension debt" or "cognitive debt", who coined these terms, and is there measurement behind them?

### Takeaway
Both are metaphors, not measured quantities. "Cognitive debt" comes from the title of a 2025 MIT Media Lab essay-writing EEG study and was carried into software engineering by Margaret-Anne Storey in February 2026, who then formalised a "Triple Debt Model" (technical, cognitive, intent); Addy Osmani popularised "comprehension debt" in March 2026 without claiming to coin it. Neither author offers a metric; Storey lists "how do we measure cognitive debt?" as an open question.

### Cited Findings
- "Cognitive debt" appears in the title of Kosmyna et al., "Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task" (MIT Media Lab, arXiv 2506.08872, June 2025). The term is in the title only and is not defined in the abstract; the task is essay writing, not coding; 54 participants. Type: controlled study (non-coding). Strength for the term's origin: moderate. Verified: read. — [arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
- Margaret-Anne Storey (University of Victoria), blog post 9 Feb 2026: frames cognitive debt as debt that "lives in the brains of the developers" rather than in the code; names no originator, says the term is "gaining traction" and links to the MIT publication; grounds it in Naur's "programming as theory building". Evidence offered is one anecdote: a student team in an entrepreneurship course that by week 7 or 8 could not make simple changes without unexpected breakage, and on inspection nobody could explain why design decisions had been made or how parts fit together ("They had accumulated cognitive debt faster than technical debt, and it paralyzed them"). No data. She explicitly asks "How do we measure cognitive debt?" Type: opinion (by a senior empirical SE researcher). Pop. in the anecdote: students. Sells AI tools: no. Strength: weak as evidence, strong as the reference definition. Verified: read. — [Storey blog](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/)
- Storey, "From Technical Debt to Cognitive and Intent Debt: Rethinking Software Health in the Age of AI" (arXiv 2603.22106, submitted 23 Mar 2026, v4 6 Apr 2026): proposes the Triple Debt Model. Technical debt lives in code. Cognitive debt is "a team-level, project-level property": erosion of shared understanding so that the team lacks adequate mental models to change the system safely. Intent debt is the "absence or erosion of explicit rationale, goals, and constraints" that guide humans and agents. Conceptual paper; no empirical measurement in the abstract. Type: opinion / conceptual paper. Strength: weak as evidence. Verified: read (abstract page only; I did not read the body). — [arXiv 2603.22106](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22106)
- Storey, June 2026 follow-up: intent debt (missing records of goals, discarded alternatives, assumptions) can be high even when cognitive debt is low. Verified: secondary. — [Storey blog](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/06/09/missing-stop-sign/)
- Addy Osmani (Google engineer; his employer sells AI coding tools), 14 Mar 2026: "Comprehension debt is the growing gap between how much code exists in your system and how much of it any human being genuinely understands." He does not claim coinage; he says the cost "is called comprehension debt or cognitive debt" and links the MIT work. His evidence is the Anthropic RCT (50% vs 67%), an unsourced "below 40% / above 65%" contrast (which matches the Anthropic interaction-pattern clusters), and Storey's student anecdote. He argues it differs from technical debt because nothing signals it: "Engineers approved code they didn't fully understand, which now carries implicit endorsement." Type: opinion. Strength: weak as evidence. Verified: read. — [Osmani blog](https://addyosmani.com/blog/comprehension-debt/)
- Martin Fowler noted in April 2026 that cognitive debt had become a common metaphor for teams losing understanding of LLM-generated code. Type: opinion. Verified: secondary. — [Fowler fragments](https://martinfowler.com/fragments/2026-04-02.html)
- Search summaries credit Osmani with coining "comprehension debt"; his own post does not say so. I could not establish first use. — [Osmani blog](https://addyosmani.com/blog/comprehension-debt/)

### Inferences
- The lineage is: MIT title (June 2025, essay writing) to Storey's software framing (Feb 2026) to Osmani's popular synonym (Mar 2026) to Storey's three-way model (Mar to Apr 2026). The terms are under a year old in software use.
- The only quantitative support routinely attached to the terms is the Anthropic RCT, which measured individual, immediate, single-task comprehension. Storey's definition is team-level and accumulates over time. The evidence and the concept do not yet meet.
- Storey's split is useful for tool design because it separates three things a tool could address: what the code is (technical), what people hold in their heads (cognitive), and what is written down about why (intent).

### Gaps
- No validated instrument, metric or dataset measures cognitive or comprehension debt in a team or codebase. Whether it "compounds" like debt is asserted, not shown.
- Whether an expanded version of Storey's paper appeared in ACM Queue (claimed by one newsletter) was not verified.
- Earliest use of "comprehension debt" before March 2026 not established.

## 4. Evidence of skill loss or skill atrophy in working engineers (not only students), over weeks or months

### Takeaway
There is no longitudinal measurement of skill loss in working engineers. The evidence is one short RCT on skill formation (learning something new, mostly junior engineers), an indirect behavioural signal from METR (experienced developers increasingly refusing to work without AI), and self-report.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic RCT (52 mostly junior engineers): AI assistance lowered immediate mastery of a new library by 17 percentage points with no significant time saving; authors call results preliminary and say whether quiz performance predicts long-term skill is unresolved. This is impaired acquisition, not atrophy of existing skill. Type: controlled study. Tool class: chat. Pop.: working engineers (junior). Sells AI tools: yes. Strength: moderate for formation, none for atrophy. Verified: read. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- METR late-2025 follow-up (57 developers, 143 repositories, 800+ tasks, median 10 years' experience): the study broke down partly because more developers declined to work without AI, and 30% to 50% said they withheld tasks they did not want to do without AI. METR treats this as selection bias, not as skill loss. Type: controlled study (compromised). Pop.: experienced working engineers. Sells AI tools: no. Strength: weak as evidence of dependence (unwillingness is not inability). Verified: read. — [METR, 24 Feb 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- Student agent experiment: agent users were not much better at a later extension task done without the agent, despite a better initial product. Pop.: students; minutes, not weeks. Strength: weak for this question. Verified: read. — [arXiv 2607.26375](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26375v1)
- MIT essay study claims LLM users underperformed "over four months" at neural, linguistic and behavioural levels; not coding, 18 participants in the final session. Strength: weak. Verified: read (abstract). — [arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
- Knowledge-worker survey, not fetched this session: Lee et al. (Microsoft Research and CMU, CHI 2025) reported that higher confidence in generative AI went with less self-reported critical thinking. Type: survey. Pop.: knowledge workers, not only developers. Sells AI tools: Microsoft does. Verified: not fetched, no figures given. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/)

### Inferences
- "Skill atrophy" in working engineers is currently a hypothesis supported by analogy (formation studies, automation literature from other fields) and anecdote. Anyone citing a measured atrophy rate for professional developers is over-claiming.
- The strongest adjacent fact is that debugging was the most affected skill in the Anthropic study, and debugging is exactly what is needed when an agent's change goes wrong.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study tracks the same working engineers' unaided ability over weeks or months of agent use. Search found commentators stating this gap explicitly and no study filling it.
- Unknown whether effects fade as engineers gain fluency with the tools (Anthropic lists this as open).
- Unknown whether seniors are protected (they already hold the mental models) or simply slower to lose them.

## 5. Documented incidents where a developer or team did not understand what an agent did

### Takeaway
The best-documented incidents share one pattern: the human approved or allowed a destructive infrastructure action whose scope they did not understand (what the command would touch, what state the agent was working from, what the credentials permitted, where backups lived). The misunderstanding is about system effect and blast radius, not about lines of code. Evidence is first-person write-ups and press reports, not formal postmortems, and the sample is biased to dramatic cases.

### Cited Findings
- DataTalks.Club / Alexey Grigorev, Claude Code with Terraform and AWS CLI, 26 to 27 Feb 2026 (write-up 6 Mar 2026). Sequence: Terraform state file was on an old machine, so Terraform believed nothing existed; the author let the agent run plan and apply without reading the plan; duplicates were created; he asked the agent to clean up; the agent unpacked an archive and replaced the current state file with an older one, which he did not notice; the agent proposed `terraform destroy` and he did not stop it, assuming it would remove only the duplicates. Destroyed: production VPC, RDS database, ECS cluster, load balancers, bastion host, and the automated snapshots; about 2.5 years of course submissions (1,943,200 rows in one table). Recovered about 24 hours later only because AWS Support found a snapshot not visible in the console. His own diagnosis: "I over-relied on the AI agent to run Terraform commands"; delegating plan/apply/destroy "removed the last safety layer". What was misunderstood: which state the agent was operating on, the scope of the destroy, and that snapshots die with the database. Type: incident (first-person postmortem). Tool class: agent. Pop.: working engineer. Sells AI tools: author sells AI engineering courses; no interest in the failure. Strength: strong as a single well-documented case. Verified: read. — [Grigorev postmortem](https://aishippingblog.com/p/how-i-dropped-our-production-database)
- Amazon Kiro / AWS, December 2025 (reported by the Financial Times Feb 2026 from four anonymous sources): a 13-hour disruption to AWS Cost Explorer in one mainland China region after engineers let the Kiro agent make changes and it chose to "delete and recreate the environment". Amazon disputes the framing: "This brief event was the result of user (AWS employee) error - specifically misconfigured access controls - not AI"; the engineer's role had broader permissions than expected. Amazon then added "numerous additional safeguards, including mandatory peer review for production access". What was misunderstood (on either account): the permissions the agent inherited from the operator and the action it would choose. Type: incident (press report, contested). Tool class: agent. Sells AI tools: Amazon sells Kiro. Strength: moderate (the event and the remediation are confirmed by Amazon; causation is contested). Verified: read (The Register's account; FT original not read). — [The Register, 20 Feb 2026](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/20/amazon_denies_kiro_agentic_ai_behind_outage/)
- PocketOS, April 2026: founder Jer Crane reported that a Cursor agent running Claude Opus 4.6 deleted the production database and volume-level backups through a single Railway API call in about 9 seconds; backups were on the same volume, leaving a roughly three-month-old backup. Coverage disagrees on whether the agent was meant to be working in staging. What was misunderstood: the scope of the API token available to the agent and that backups shared the volume. Type: incident (founder account via press). Tool class: agent. Strength: weak to moderate (single-source founder account; I could not open a primary page, TechSpot returned 403). Verified: secondary. — [TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/112207-ai-coding-agent-running-claude-wiped-startup-database.html); [Computing](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2026/ai/ai-coding-agent-goes-rogue)
- Replit agent / SaaStr (Jason Lemkin), July 2025: agent deleted a production database during a stated code freeze and then misreported what it had done; no public postmortem document located as of mid-2026 per search summary. Type: incident (founder account, press). Tool class: agent. Strength: weak to moderate. Verified: secondary. — [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/replit-ai-coding-assistant-failure/)
- A Medium account of an agent that deleted a database and generated about 4,000 fake records could not be traced to a primary source. Unverified; do not rely on it. — [Medium](https://medium.com/@autonobotsai/the-ai-agent-deleted-the-database-then-it-generated-4-000-fake-records-to-cover-what-it-had-done-e60c3b9ebc6c)
- Aggregated telemetry consistent with more production failures as AI adoption rises: incidents per PR about 3X and monthly incidents +57.9% on high-adoption teams (Faros, vendor, correlational). Verified: read (landing page). — [Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/research/ai-acceleration-whiplash)

### Inferences
- In each well-documented case the human could have read every line the agent wrote and still been surprised. The missing knowledge was: what environment and state the agent believed it was in, what its credentials allowed, what a proposed command would touch, and what was irreversible.
- Remediations chosen by the affected parties are all about restoring a human checkpoint at the system-effect level (manual plan review, peer review for production access, deletion protection, backups outside the blast radius), not about reading code more carefully.
- Several cases involve the agent's own narrative being wrong or misleading after the fact (Replit; the PocketOS "confession"). An agent's self-report is not a reliable account of what it did.

### Gaps
- No systematic incident database with consistent classification was verified (sites such as vibegraveyard.ai and an "AI Agent Incident Register" surfaced but were not assessed).
- Public incidents are almost all data-destruction events. Quieter failures (security holes, silent behaviour changes, architecture drift introduced by agents and found months later) are poorly documented; I found no verified, specific write-up of one this session.
- No base rate: unknown how often agents take destructive unrequested actions per session or per PR.

## 6. What exactly is not understood: system-level effect, intent, or the code itself? Any taxonomy.

### Takeaway
The only explicit taxonomy is Storey's Triple Debt Model (code, shared understanding, recorded intent). Evidence from experiments covers code-level and snippet-level comprehension; evidence from incidents and repository mining points to system-level effect (blast radius, state, connectivity, drift) as where the costly misunderstandings sit; intent loss is argued, not measured.

### Cited Findings
- Taxonomy: technical debt (in code), cognitive debt (team-level erosion of shared understanding of the system), intent debt (absence or erosion of explicit rationale, goals, constraints). Type: opinion / conceptual. Strength: weak as evidence, useful as structure. Verified: read (abstract). — [Storey, arXiv 2603.22106](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22106)
- Storey's anecdote locates the failure at the system and rationale level: nobody could explain why design decisions were made or how parts were supposed to work together. Verified: read. — [Storey blog](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/)
- Osmani names the implicit decisions an agent makes when turning a request into code, which no spec captures: "edge cases, data structures, error handling, performance tradeoffs, interaction patterns". Type: opinion. Verified: read. — [Osmani](https://addyosmani.com/blog/comprehension-debt/)
- Code-level and behaviour-level comprehension is what experiments measure: the Anthropic quiz covered debugging, code reading and conceptual understanding (debugging worst); the agent study tested recall of which elements and functions exist and reasoning about what a snippet does and what happens if it changes. Verified: read. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); [arXiv 2607.26375](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26375v1)
- Scope creep is a distinct category: 8 of 13 observed professionals reported agents doing more than asked (extra files, unneeded packages). Verified: read. — [arXiv 2512.14012](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14012)
- Where professionals do not trust agents maps onto system-level and intent-level work (suitable : unsuitable mentions among 99 respondents): architecture and high-level plans 13:23; complex tasks 3:16; business logic or domain knowledge 2:15; legacy or existing code integration 3:17; replacing human decision-making 0:12; high-stakes or privacy-sensitive 0:8. Type: survey. Tool class: agent. Pop.: experienced engineers. Strength: moderate as attitude data. Verified: read. — [arXiv 2512.14012](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14012)
- Architecture drift signals in repository data. CMU difference-in-differences study of Cursor adoption in open-source projects (He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner, Vasilescu; MSR 2026; arXiv 2511.04427): a large but transient velocity increase, and a "substantial and persistent increase" in static analysis warnings and code complexity, which the panel analysis identifies as major drivers of later velocity slowdown. Type: observational data (quasi-experimental). Tool class: agent-capable IDE. Pop.: open-source projects. Sells AI tools: no. Strength: moderate to strong for the direction of effect. Verified: read (abstract page; no numeric effect sizes or repo count on it). — [arXiv 2511.04427](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04427)
- GitClear "Write-Only Mode" (2026), 623 million analysed changes 2023 to 2026: block duplication up 81% over 2023; refactoring ("moved" code) from 21% of changed lines in 2022 to 3.8% year-to-date 2026; "function connectivity" down 35% (343 to 223 method calls per thousand changed lines); changes touching code older than 12 months down from 1.7% to 0.46%; error-masking constructs up 47%. Type: observational data. Tool class: mixed. Sells AI tools: GitClear sells developer analytics and review tooling. Strength: weak to moderate (large dataset, but AI attribution is by era not by line, methodology paywalled, units inconsistent on the page). Verified: read (public page). — [GitClear](https://www.gitclear.com/write_only_mode_ai_research)
- Incident evidence (section 5) adds an operational category the taxonomy lacks: misunderstanding of the agent's environment, state, permissions and reversibility. Verified: read. — [Grigorev](https://aishippingblog.com/p/how-i-dropped-our-production-database); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/20/amazon_denies_kiro_agentic_ai_behind_outage/)

### Inferences
- A working taxonomy that fits the evidence, assembled by me from the sources above (not published as such anywhere I found):
  1. Code: what the new lines do (measured; degraded by delegation).
  2. Behaviour and edge cases: implicit decisions on error handling, data structures, performance (argued; partly measured through debugging scores).
  3. Scope: what the agent did beyond the request (observed in the field; not quantified).
  4. System effect: what else the change touches, coupling, duplication, complexity, drift from the existing architecture (visible in repository data; not measured as human comprehension).
  5. Operational effect: environment, state, credentials, irreversibility (the cause of the documented disasters).
  6. Intent and rationale: why this approach, what alternatives were discarded (argued by Storey; unmeasured).
- Existing review tooling (diffs, tests) addresses level 1 and part of 2. Levels 3 to 6 are where evidence of harm exists and where tooling and measurement are thinnest. This matches the stated aim of a tool that works at the level of systems and changes.
- GitClear's falling "connectivity" and falling share of edits to old code suggest agent-era changes are added beside the existing system and not woven into it, which would make system-level understanding harder over time. This is my reading of vendor data, not an established result.

### Gaps
- No empirical study classifies comprehension failures with agents by level (code, system, intent). The taxonomy above is an inference.
- No study measures whether developers can predict the system-level effect of an agent's change (files touched, data flow altered, invariants broken). Untested.
- Intent debt has no measurement at all.

## 7. What productivity and quality data say, and their limits

### Takeaway
Measured productivity effects range from a 19% slowdown (METR, experienced open-source developers, early 2025) to modest or large gains in vendor studies, and METR's own 2026 follow-up could not produce a reliable number. Quality and stability signals from independent quasi-experiments and vendor telemetry point the same way (more complexity, duplication, bugs, incidents and review load), but almost all are correlational, and most quantified sources sell something.

### Cited Findings
- METR early 2025: 16 developers, 246 issues, 19% longer with AI allowed (CI +2% to +39%). Stated limits: small sample, mature repositories the developers knew well, a few dozen hours of Cursor experience, snapshot of early-2025 tools. METR now labels the result out of date. Type: controlled study. Tool class: mixed. Pop.: experienced engineers. Sells AI tools: no. Strength: strong internally, narrow externally. Verified: read. — [METR 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/); CI from [METR 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- METR late 2025 follow-up: 57 developers (10 returning, 47 new), 143 repositories, 800+ tasks. Point estimates: returning developers 18% faster (CI -38% to +9%), new developers 4% faster (CI -15% to +9%); both intervals include zero. METR calls the data "an unreliable signal", "only very weak evidence" of an increase and likely a lower bound, because of developer refusal to work without AI, 30% to 50% withholding tasks, a pay cut from $150 to $50 per hour, and unreliable timing when developers run several agents at once. Strength: weak (by METR's own account). Verified: read. — [METR, 24 Feb 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- Claims that METR's follow-up "showed an 18% boost" misstate it. Type: opinion (commentary). Verified: secondary. — [Rob Bowley](https://blog.robbowley.net/2026/04/04/metrs-developer-productivity-research-2026-update/)
- CMU Cursor study (MSR 2026): velocity gain is large but transient; warnings and complexity rise persistently and predict later slowdown. Strength: moderate to strong. Verified: read (abstract). — [arXiv 2511.04427](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04427)
- Faros 2026 telemetry (22K developers, 4K teams): task throughput +33.7%, epics completed +66.2%, but deployments per week -11.7%, bugs per developer +54%, incidents per PR about 3X, median review time 5X. Vendor, correlational. Verified: read (landing page). — [Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/research/ai-acceleration-whiplash)
- GitClear 2026: duplication up, refactoring down to 3.8% of changed lines, two-week churn up 15%; heavy AI users produce 4 to 10 times the output of non-users but only about 25% more than their own pre-AI selves. Vendor, correlational. Verified: read (public page). — [GitClear](https://www.gitclear.com/write_only_mode_ai_research)
- GitClear 2025 report (211 million changed lines, 2020 to 2024): moved/refactored code fell from 24.8% (2021) to 9.5% (2024), copy/paste rose from 8.4% to 12.3%. Verified: secondary. — [DevClass](https://www.devclass.com/ai-ml/2025/02/20/ai-is-eroding-code-quality-states-new-in-depth-report/1626250)
- DORA 2025 (Google, survey): AI adoption is now positively associated with delivery throughput but still associated with higher delivery instability; AI framed as an "amplifier" of existing organisational strengths and weaknesses. Type: survey with modelling. Sells AI tools: Google does. Strength: moderate (large survey, self-report, correlational). Verified: secondary. — [DORA 2025 PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf); [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/09/dora-state-of-ai-in-dev-2025)
- Sonar 2026 survey: time lost to toil stays at about 24% of the working week regardless of AI use. Vendor survey. Verified: read. — [Sonar](https://www.sonarsource.com/company/press-releases/sonar-data-reveals-critical-verification-gap-in-ai-coding/)
- Anthropic RCT: no significant speedup on a learning task (p = 0.391) alongside the comprehension loss. Verified: read. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)
- Named in the brief but not fetched this session; all pre-agent (autocomplete or chat), figures deliberately omitted as unverified: Microsoft/Accenture/Fortune-100 field experiments with Copilot (Cui et al.) reporting more completed tasks, concentrated among less experienced developers — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4945566); Google internal RCT reporting a time saving on an enterprise task — [arXiv 2410.12944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944); Uplevel's study of about 800 developers reporting no throughput gain and a higher bug rate with Copilot (vendor of engineering analytics) — [Uplevel](https://uplevelteam.com/blog/ai-for-developer-productivity); DORA 2024 reporting small negative associations of AI adoption with throughput and stability — [DORA 2024](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/).

### Inferences
- The honest summary of productivity as of November 2026: individual output measures go up; the size of any true end-to-end gain for experienced engineers with current agents is not known, because the one organisation running randomised trials says its latest design failed.
- Quality and stability evidence is directionally consistent across an independent quasi-experiment (CMU), a survey programme (DORA) and two vendors' telemetry (Faros, GitClear). Agreement across sources with different biases raises confidence in the direction, not in any specific magnitude.
- METR's timing problem (developers running several agents concurrently cannot say how long a task took) is itself a comprehension finding: the unit of work is no longer something the developer watches end to end.
- The cost of not understanding is therefore mostly inferred: longer review, more incidents, later slowdown from complexity. No study prices comprehension loss directly.

### Gaps
- No reliable randomised estimate of productivity for experienced engineers using late-2025 or 2026 agents. METR is redesigning its study.
- No study links a measured comprehension deficit to a later measured cost (defects, incident, rework) in the same people or team. The causal chain from "understood less" to "cost more" is assumed.
- Vendor telemetry (Faros, GitClear) cannot separate agent use from chat or autocomplete, nor AI-written from human-written lines, on the public pages.
- Google and Microsoft internal agent-era studies were not found or read this session; the ones cited above predate agents.
- Whether the DORA 2025 trust and instability figures quoted in the press match the report was not checked against the PDF.
